# Overview of Ethical Hacking

Overview of Ethical Hacking

These days, it’s hard to look at any source of news without seeing something about data theft, Internet-based crime, or various other cyberattacks against people and businesses of all sizes and sectors. What we see in the news, actually, are the big issues, with large numbers of records compromised or big companies breached. What you don’t see is the number of system compromises where the target of the attack is someone’s personal computer or other device. Consider, for example, the Mirai botnet, which infected smaller, special-purpose devices running an embedded implementation of \*nix. The number of devices thought to have been compromised and made part of that botnet is well over 100,000 with the possibility of there being more than one million.

Each year, millions of new pieces of malware are created, often making use of new vulnerabilities that have been discovered. Since 2005, there has not been a year without at least 10 million data records compromised. In the year 2023, nearly 200 million records were compromised. These numbers are just from the United States alone. To put this into perspective, there are only about 250 million adults in the United States, so it’s safe to say that every adult has had their information compromised or breached at some point. To be clear, the data records that we’re talking about belong to individual people and not to businesses. There is minimal accounting of the total value of intellectual property that may have been stolen, but it’s clear that the cycle of compromise has been ongoing for quite some time.

All of this is to say there is an urgen need to improve how information security is handled. It’s believed that to protect against cyberattacks, you have to be able to understand those attacks, and the adversarial mindframe of those attacks. Ideally, you need to replicate the attacks. If businesses are testing attacks against their own infrastructure early and often, thos businesses could be in a better position to improve their defenses and keep the real adversaries out.

This type of testing is what ethical hacking essentially comprises. It is all about ferreting out problems with a goal of improving the overall security posture of a target. This may be for a company in terms of their infrastructure or even desktop systems. It may also be performing testing against software to identify bugs that can be used to compromise the software and, subsequently, the system where the software is housed. The aim is not to be malicious, but to be on the ‘good’ side to improve a critical situation. This is something you could be hired or contracted to perform for a business. They may have a set of systems or web applications they want tested and assessed. You could also have software that needs to be tested. There are a lot of people who perform testing on software – both commercial and open-source.

Ethical hacking can be performed under many different aliases. You may not always see the term _ethical hacking/hacker_, especially when you are looking at job titles. Instead, you will see the term _penetration testing/tester._ It’s essentially the same thing. The idea of a penetration test is to attempt to penetrate the defenses of an organization. That may also be the goal of an ethical hacker. You may also see the term _red team/red teaming/red teamer_, which is generally considered a specific type of penetration test where ethe testers are adversarial to the organization and network under testing and assessment. A red teamer would actually simulate the role of a malicious actor, meaning they would try to be stealthy so as to not be detected.

One of the challenging aspect of this sort of activity is having to think like an adversary. Testing of this nature is often challenging and requires a different way of thinking. When doing any sort of testing, including ethical hacking, a methodology is important to follow, as it helps ensure that your actions are both repeatable and verifiable. There are a number of methodologies you may come across. Security professionals who have been doing this type of work for an extensive time may have developed their own methodology; however, they will often follow common steps, such as the ones I am going to illustrate as we move throughout the chapter. A methodical approach and goal of ethical hacking is to adhere to a set of standards ensuring that ethical hackers behave ethically, in service to their employers. They are expected to not do harm and to work toward improving the security posture rather than doing damage to the posture.

Methodologies

As with so many things, using a methodology is valuable when it comes to ethical hacking or security assessment and testing. Methodologies help with consistency, repeatability, and rprocess improvements. Consistency is key and is important because you want to be able to run the sa,e set of tests or probes no matter who you are testing against. Let’s say, for example, you are working with a company that keeps asking you back. Without consistency, you may miss some critical findings from one test to another, which may let them thin they got better, or the finding doesn’t exist any longer. This would leave a bad impression to leave a company with. Similarly, repeatability give you the ability to do the same tests every time you run an assessment. In fact, if you are working with a team, every one of you should be able to run the set of tests. Again, you want to be sure that any organization you are assessing will have the same perspective in their security posture, no matter how many times they come to you to run the series of tests and no matter how the organization is.

There are some testing or assessment methodologies that get used throughout the cyber industry. They are typically built around expectations of what an adversary would do. While this may or may not be realistic, there are some other ways of viewing how adversaries operate that are useful and beyond helpful to understand. The first is the Lockheed Martin Cyber Kill Chain (CKC), and the other is the hacker attack lifecycle. When we get to look at the methodology of ethical hacking, you will see the similarities and differences.

The Cyber Kill Chain

A commonly referred-to framework in the information security space is the cyber kill chain. A kill chain is a military concept of the structure of an attack. The idea of a kill chain is that you can identify where the adversary is at in their process so you can adapt your own response tactics. Lockheed Martin, a defense contractor, adapted the military concept of a kill chain to the information security (or cybersecurity) space.
