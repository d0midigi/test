# History of Hacking

History of Hacking

Many of today’s news stories related to cybersecurity focus on attackers—what they do and the consequences of their actions. In this text, we will cover a wide range of techniques and technologies that attackers use to compromise systems. We will also consider how security professionals can use those same techniques and technologies to make their systems harder to attack. But before we dive into the details, it is important to first gain an understanding of who these attackers are and why they do what they do.

During the early generations of digital computing (way back in the 1960s), learning about computing wasn’t nearly as easy as it is today. In many cases, the best way to learn was to build your own computer! From this do-it-yourself era, a group of individuals emerged who were passionately interested in learning all they could about computers. They learned about hardware, software, and ways to connect devices and communicate. Their often-imprecise methods of building and accessing devices earned them the moniker hackers. The first generation of hackers were individuals who are called “geeks” or technology enthusiasts today. These early hackers went on to create the foundation for technologies such as the Advanced Research Projects Agency Network (ARPANET), which paved the way for the Internet. They also initiated many early software-development movements that led to what is known today as open source. Their hacking was motivated by intellectual curiosity and a quest to collectively expand the body of knowledge for this new domain of digital computing; causing damage or stealing information was “against the rules” for this core group of people.

In the 1980s, hackers started to gain more of a negative reputation —one with which the public now identifies them. Movies such as WarGames and media attention to their exploits altered the image of a hacker from a technology enthusiast to a computer criminal. During this time, hackers engaged in activities such as theft of service by breaking into phone systems to make free phone calls. Books such as _The Cuckoo’s Egg_ and the emergence of magazines such as _Phrack_ cast even more negative light on hackers. In many respects, the 1980s formed the basis for how a hacker is perceived today.

Fortunately, the overall image of hackers is not all bad. A new generation of security professionals, who share many of the same aspirations as the original hackers, have emerged to use their knowledge and skills to protect systems, rather than to compromise them. These professionals use hacking for ethical purposes, identifying vulnerabilities and helping to strengthen security measures to defend against malicious attacks. Their work is vital in an increasingly digital world, as they safeguard sensitive information and maintain the integrity of critical cybersecurity infrastructure.

Despite these positive developments, the persistent and evolving nature of cyber threats ensures that hackers, both malicious and ethical, are not going away anytime soon. As technology continues to advance, so too will the techniques and strategies employed by cybercriminals, and so too will the accidents and mistakes that script kiddies can make not knowing how to correctly operate a tool. These mistakes often create additional vulnerabilities or cause unintended damage, resulting in consequences and leaving ethical hackers with the crucial task of fixing these problems. This ongoing battle between attackers and defenders highlights the importance of continuous learning and adaptation within the cybersecurity field. The presence of both malicious hackers and ethical security professionals will remain a fundamental aspect of the digital landscape, driving innovation and the implementation of ever-more sophisticated defenses.

With that being said, we must remember that as ethical hackers, we set a good example and pave the way for future ethical hackers and cybersecurity defenders. If we don’t provide ethical guidance, who will they have to look up to? Without strong role models, the world could become overrun with black hats, causing widespread chaos and undermining the progress and security of humankind. By fostering a culture of integrity and responsibility, we can help ensure that the next generation of ethical hackers is equipped not only with the technical skills but also the ethical framework necessary to protect and enhance our digital world long after we are gone.

### Profiles and Motives of Different Types of Hackers

Over the past four decades, the definition of a hacker has evolved quite a bit from the definition accepted in the 1980s and even the 1990s. Current hackers defy easy classification and are best understood by looking at the motivations for their actions. Although there is no comprehensive list of the types of hackers active in today’s global online environments, here is a general list of categories of their motivations (you’ll learn more about each type of hacker in a later section in this chapter):

* **White Hat Hackers:** Information security (InfoSec) professionals use their skills to identify and fix security vulnerabilities, protecting systems from potential breaches and harm. They operate with permission and authorization from system owners and follow ethical guidelines to ensure their activities are legal and constructive. Often employed by organizations to conduct ethical hacking security assessments and penetration testing engagements, and security audits, white hat hackers play a crucial role in securing cybersecurity infrastructure.
* **Gray Hat Hackers:** A gray hat hacker is someone who falls between the categories of black hats and white hat hackers. Unlike black hat hackers, who engage in illegal activities for personal gain or malicious intent, and white hat hackers, who seek to improve security by identifying vulnerabilities and informing the affected parties, gray hat hackers often exploit vulnerabilities without permission but typically with no malicious intent either. They may expose security flaws publicly to pressure organizations to fix them or to gain recognition, which can lead to ethical and legal debates about their actions.
* **Black Hat Hackers:** A black hat hacker, on the other hand, engages in illegal and unethical activities to exploit security weaknesses for personal gain or malicious purposes. These hackers may steal data, deploy malware, disrupt services, or cause damage to systems and networks. Motivated by financial profit, political agendas, or the thrill of the attack, black hat hackers operate without the consent or authorization of the affected parties and often seek to avoid detection and prosecution. Their actions pose significant risks to individuals, organizations, and governments, leading to financial losses, reputational damage, and compromised security postures.
* **The Lone Wolf:** A lone wolf hacker operates independently, without affiliation to any group or organization. These hackers prefer to work solo, drive by personal motives which can range from curiosity and the thrill of the challenge to ideological beliefs or financial gain. The independence of a lone wolf hacker makes them unpredictable, as they rely solely on their own skills and resources. This autonomy allows them to be highly flexible and adaptable in their approaches to hacking.
* **Suicide Hackers:** A suicide hacker is an individual who carries out cyberattacks with the full understanding that their actions will likely lead to severe and grave consequences, including legal penalties or personal harm. Unlike other hackers who take measures to conceal their identities and avoid detection, suicide hackers accept or even embrace the likelihood of being caught. Their motives are often driven by a desire to make a significant impact or personal statement, regardless of the personal cost. This term can apply to those engaging in politically or ideologically motivated attacks.
* **Script Kiddie, or Skiddz:** Amateurs; entry-level hackers who do not possess their own advanced skills but rather use only scripts and software written by more experienced hackers. Because amateurs rarely do more than run other people’s scripts, they are often called “script kiddies.”
* **Ideologues**, **or Hacktivists:** Hackers who carry out their activities to achieve ideological or political goals. These types of hackers are sometimes called “hacktivists” because they use their hacking skills to carry out activist activities.
* **Nation-State:** An increasingly worrisome group of cybercriminals are those who are directly trained and supported by nation-states. Nation-state actors are extremely sophisticated, have large budgets, and are tasked with carrying out cyberwarfare operations.
* **State-Sponsored:** A state-sponsored hacker operates under the direction or support of a government. These hackers are typically part of a larger, organized group working on behalf of their nation to conduct cyber espionage, sabotage, or informational warfare. Their activities are aimed at gaining intelligence, disrupting services, or weakening the capabilities of other nations. State-sponsored hackers have access to significant resources and advanced tools, making them particularly formidable adversaries in the cyber domain.
* **Elitist Hackers:** An elite hacker is a highly skilled individual within the hacking community, often recognized for their advanced technical abilities and extensive knowledge of pretty much all facets of cybersecurity. These hackers can develop sophisticated exploits, discovering zero-day vulnerabilities, and evading detection mechanisms. Elite hackers may work in various capacities, from contributing to cybersecurity research and development to engaging in high-profile cyberattacks. Their expertise sets them apart from the average hacker, and they are often sought after for their ability to tackle complex and challenging security problems.

Subtle Nuances Between a State-Sponsored and Nation-State Hacker: What Are The Differences?

The terms **state-sponsored hacker** and **nation-state hacker** are often used interchangeably but can have nuanced differences based on context, or who you talk to.

A **state-sponsored hacker** refers to an individual or group that conducts cyber activities on behalf of a government or its agencies. These hackers are typically part of or supported by a government, receiving regular funding, resources, and directives to carry out specific targeted missions. Their activities can include:

* Cyber espionage
* Intellectual properties theft
* Sabotage
* Cyber terrorism and cyber warfare

Their attacks are specifically aimed at advancing the sponsoring state’s strategic objectives and agenda. State-sponsored hackers may or may not be formal members of the government but work closely under its guidance.

A **nation-state hacker**, on the other hand, specifically denotes a hacker or hacking group that acts directly under the orders and auspices of a country’s government. These hackers are usually embedded withing governmental institutions, such as military units, intelligence agencies, or specialized cyber warfare divisions. Nation-state hackers have direct links to their government and typically have access to extensive resources, advanced technologies, and classified intelligence. Their operations are often more strategic, planned out, and aligned with the broader geopolitical goals of the nation.

Most of today’s organizations have quickly learned that they can no longer afford to underestimate or ignore the growing threat that increasingly sophisticated attackers pose. Organizations of all sizes have learned to reduce threats through a combination of technical, administrative, and physical measures designed to address a specific range of problems. Technical measures include devices and techniques such as virtual private networks (VPNs), cryptographic protocols, intrusion detection systems (IDSs) and intrusion prevention systems (IPSs), access control lists (ACLs), biometrics, smart cards, and other devices. Administrative controls include policies, procedures, and other rules. Physical measures include devices such as cable locks, device locks, alarm systems, and other similar devices. Although any of these devices or controls may be expensive, they will likely be cheaper and more effective than the cost and effort required to clean up after a successful attack.

While discussing attacks and attackers, InfoSec professionals must be thorough when assessing and evaluating threats by also considering where those threats originate. When evaluating the threats against an organization and possible sources of attack, always consider the fact that attackers can come from both outside and inside the organization. Given the nearly global access to the Internet, “outside” an organization can mean literally anywhere in the world today. External attackers may attempt to hide their attacks by initiating them from far-away countries that may have very different laws than those in the victim’s country. Meanwhile, internal attackers can use their insider knowledge to focus their attacks. A single disgruntled employee can cause tremendous damage because that person is an authorized user of the system. Although you will likely see many more external attacks, the actions of a malicious insider may go unnoticed longer, and such an individual may have some level of knowledge of how things work ahead of time, which can result in a more effective attack.

Controls

Each organization is responsible for protecting itself from risks by determining the controls that will be most effective in reducing or mitigating the threats it faces. One approach to developing a balanced and effective strategy for selecting security controls is the TAP principle. TAP is an acronym for technical, administrative, and physical—the three types of controls you can use to mitigate risk. Here’s a look at each type, with a few examples:

* **Technical**: Technical controls take the form of software or hardware devices, such as firewalls, proxies, IDSs, IPSs, biometric **authentication**, permissions, auditing, and similar technologies.
* **Administrative:** Administrative controls take the form of policies and procedures. Examples include a password policy that defines what makes a strong password and an acceptable use policy (AUP) that defines acceptable and unacceptable uses for an organization’s computers and devices. In many cases, administrative controls also fulfill legal requirements, such as policies that dictate privacy of customer information. Other examples of administrative policy include the rules governing actions taken when hiring and firing employees.
* **Physical**: Physical controls protect assets from traditional threats such as theft or vandalism. Mechanisms in this category include door locks, cameras, security guards, lighting, fences, gates, and other similar devices.

The Mind Frame of a Hacker

Depending on whom you ask, you will most likely receive a wide range of responses from fellow hackers about how they view their actions. In fact, many malicious hackers, like other individuals who break rules or laws for various personal reasons, have their own code of ethics that they hold near and dear, or sacred. In defense of their actions, hackers have been known to cite various justifications, including the following:

* **The Concept of Victimless Crimes:** This suggests that if humans are not direct targets, committing the crime is not inherently wrong. This rationale, however, does not apply to attacks that do target individuals.
* **The Robing Hood Ethos:** This ethos proposes that stealing software and media from affluent companies and distributing them to less privileged customers, often through methods like BitTorrent and PirateBay, that circumvent intellectual property protections, is acceptable because these companies are perceived as already wealthy and they’re not going to:

1\. notice the software that’s being pirated.

2\. simply won’t care that it’s missing.

3\. they make so much money that to pay a hefty penalization fee would be mere pennies to them.

* **National Pride and Patriotism:** Sometimes national pride and patriotism can inspire hackers to disrupt the balance of national or international powers. They may hack to undermine adversaries’ processes or to bolster their own country’s reputation, akin to an anti-establishment Robin Hood mentality.
* **The Educational Value of Hacking:** The educational value of hacking suggests that committing a crime is justifiable if done for learning purposes.
* **Curiosity Killed the Cat:** Curiosity-driven hacking posits that infiltrating a network is permissible if nothing is stolen or altered.

Another perspective on hacker motivations is embodied in a set of standards known as the “_**hacker ethics,”**_ and dates to the 1980s when author, Steven Levy, went public with his publication. In the preface of his book _Hackers: Heroes of the Computer Revolution,_ Levy states the following:

Motivations of Hackers

When it comes to understanding hackers, ethics plays a crucial role, but they are not the only factor to consider. Motivation is also a key aspect to explore. In the world of crime-solving television shows, it is commonly understood that three elements are necessary to commit a crime:

1. **Means:** Does the hacker have the capability to carry out the crime?
2. **Motive:** Does the hacker have a reason to commit the crime?
3. **Opportunity:** Does the hacker have the necessary access and time to commit the crime?

Moreover, examining the second point, motive, allows us to dig deeper into why an adversary may engage in hacking activities. In the early days of hacking, individuals were driven by curiosity. Today, adversaries may have a variety of motives, many of which mirror those behind traditional physical-world crimes.

1. **Beneficial Contribution:** Hackers with this motive, such as white hat hackers or ethical hackers, are not criminals. They are Information Security (InfoSec) and cybersecurity professionals who use hacking techniques to enhance the security of their organization’s systems and interconnected networks.

By simulating attacks, they identify vulnerabilities that can be addressed before malicious actors exploit them. The key distinctions between ethical hackers and unethical hackers are that the former have authorization to conduct their activities and do so to bolster an organization’s security.

1. **Status/Validation:** As novice hackers enter the scene, they often rely on pre-written scripts and programs developed by more experienced hackers, or _“elders.”_ These tools require minimal technical expertise, enabling inexperienced hackers, known as _**“script kiddies,”**_ or _**“skiddz,”**_ to cause harm. As they gain proficiency, they may customize existing exploits and eventually create their own malicious software.

Many hackers today seek recognition and notoriety in the hacking communities. Each step in their journey towards expertise brings them closer to establishing a reputation for themselves. In trying to make a name for themselves, each successful attack gives them more status and elevates their reputation in the eyes of others, often more established hackers. For many hackers, this recognition is reward enough – at least at first, or for some.

1. **Monetary Gain:** Many malicious attacks in today’s cyberworld are orchestrated with the goal of either generating revenue for an adversary or disrupting the revenue streams of the target. These attacks can involve gaining access to financial assets or valuable data for ransom. Money serves as the root of all evil, as it often does, and serves as the primary driving force for these adversaries, who may include malicious insiders, individual criminals, organized crime groups and syndicates, or cyber mercenaries.
2. **Ideological Motivations:** Unethical hackers who are motivated by ideology leverage technology to pursue their ideological objectives. Those who carry out activist attacks using malicious software are often referred to as _**"hacktivists.”**_ This category also encompasses nationalists and nation-state actors who are driven by political agendas.

These individuals often possess advanced technical skills and significant financial resources, making them some of the most formidable cybercriminals. Their sophisticated and well-funded attacks have the potential to have far-reaching global repercussions.

Exploring the Evolution of Computer Hacking

The history of computer hacking is a fascinating journey that began with early hackers driven by pure curiosity and a desire to understand the capabilities of emerging technologies. In the 1970s, hacking was limited to mainframes in corporate and university environments; however, with the rise of personal computers in the 1980s, hacking took on a new dimension as hackers realized the potential to exploit vulnerabilities on a larger scale.

One significant milestone in the evolution of hacking was the introduction of the first Internet worm by Robert T. Morris Jr. in 1988. This exploit targeted a weakness in the UNIX sendmail command, making a shift towards PC-based hacking. As hackers honed their skills and creativity, their attacks became more sophisticated, leading to the first of website defacements by 1995.

While early hacks were often characterized by humor, some took a more offensive or vulgar tone. By 2001, the frequency of website hacks had reached such levels that tracking them became a daunting task for intelligence agencies. The turn of the century saw a shift from hacking as a mere prank to malicious activities with real-world consequences.

Denial of Service (DoS) attacks emerged as a common tactic, disrupting companies’ internet access and causing financial harm. As websites began processing more credit card transactions, malicious actors targeted their databases for financial gain. The introduction of computer crime laws further incentivized hacking for profit, leading skilled hackers to offer their services to the highest bidder, including criminal organizations and hostile nations.

Furthermore, as technology, malicious actors, and countermeasures have advanced, the types of attacks and strategies employed have also evolved. Black hats have introduced new threats in the form of mutating worms, spam, spyware, adware, and rootkits, moving beyond mere annoyances to causing widespread disruptions by targeting the techniques society relies on.

Adversaries have recognized the potential to profit from their skills in various ways. For instance, adversaries have utilized techniques to redirect web browsers to pages that generate revenue for themselves, while spammers inundate inboxes with thousands of emails promoting products and services. The low cost of bulk email distribution means that even a small number of purchases can yield significant profits.

Over the past two decades, the hacking communities have shifted towards a more collaborative approach. While solitary hackers were once common, there is now a trend towards collective efforts and forces. Working in teams allows hackers to achieve greater results by leveraging their numbers, diverse skills, and clear leadership structures. Additionally, there is a concerning trend of unethical hackers receiving funding from underground and black market criminal organizations, terrorists, or foreign governments.

The proliferation of technology and society’s increasing reliance on it have made it an attractive target for cybercriminals. This underscores the importance of robust cybersecurity measures to protect against evolving threats and safeguard critical systems and information.

Ethical Hacking and Penetration Testing

Ethical Hacking and Penetration Testing are two crucial terms that InfoSec professionals encounter early in their careers. In today's InfoSec community, there are varying interpretations of these terms, making it essential to distinguish and clarify their meanings to grasp their significance in the broader context.

FYI – FOR YOUR INFORMATION

Participating in any hacking activities without the explicit permissions or authorization of the target owner is considered a criminal offense, regardless of whether you are apprehended. The only way to demonstrate that you have obtained explicit permission is by obtaining written consent before initiating any actions. Information Security professionals commonly refer to this written authorization as their _**“get out of jail free card.”**_

Contrary to common belief, hacking can be conducted legally and for beneficial purposes. For instance, when a network owner authorizes an InfoSec professional to hack into systems to identify vulnerabilities that need to be addressed. It is imperative to note the key phrases "network owner contracts" and "explicit permission" in this context. Ethical hackers operate with written consent from the asset owner, ensuring that their activities are conducted within legal and ethical boundaries.

Once granted the necessary permissions and contracts, can ethical hackers legally engage in hacking and penetration testing. This structured and methodical process involves investigating, identifying, attacking, and reporting on a target system’s strengths and vulnerabilities. When conducted under the right circumstances, penetration testing can provide valuable information to system owners to adjust their security defenses properly and appropriately.

Penetration testing can be conducted in the form of black-box or white-box testing, depending on the evaluation criteria and organizational goals. Black-box testing is utilized to simulate how a real-world cybercriminal views a system, with no prior knowledge provided to the testing team. Conversely, white-box testing involves providing advanced knowledge to the testing team, in both cases, simulated attacks are carried out to assess the potential impact of real attacks on an organization. Penetration tests are often used as part of a broader evaluation of the effectiveness of Information Technology (IT) system controls that protect an organization. It is important to note that penetration testing differs from vulnerability assessments. While the primary goal of a penetration test is to determine if a specific resource can be compromised, vulnerability assessments aim to identify as many vulnerabilities as possible within a system. Although penetration testing may be conducted alongside a vulnerability assessment, these activities are distinct exercises.

It is crucial to bear in mind that the key differentiator between black hats (individuals who engage in malicious hacking activities) and white hats (security professionals who utilize hacking techniques to safeguard systems) is adherence to legal and ethical standards.

One effective method to enhance the security of an environment is through conducting an IT security audit. This audit is typically performed against a set standard or checklist that encompasses security protocols, software development, administrative policies, and IT governance. Its purpose is to assess whether the organization’s implemented controls align with the security policy, as well as to evaluate the security policy for compliance with best practices, regulations, and legislation; however, passing an IT audit does not guarantee absolute security, as audit checklists often lag behind new attack methods by months or even years sometimes.

The Role and Responsibilities of Ethical Hacking

Ethical hackers play a crucial role in cybersecurity by utilizing their skills, training, and understanding of hacker mindset to simulate hostile attacks. To effectively defend against aggressors, one must comprehend their thought processes, actions, and reactions. This concept is akin to military training exercises where elite units are trained in the tactics of hostile nations to prepare other units for combat without risking lives.

### Key Points About Ethical Hacking

1. Ethical hacking necessitates explicit permission (preferably in written form with both parties’ signatures) from the “victim” before any activities can commence.
2. Participants employ similar tactics, strategies, and tools as malicious hackers to simulate real-world attack scenarios.
3. Ethical hacking can potentially cause harm to systems if not executed with proper care.
4. Ethical hackers must possess advanced knowledge of techniques employed by malicious hackers.
5. Rules of engagement or guidelines should be established prior to any ethical hacking activities.

Ethical hacking or penetration testing, when executed under the right circumstances with proper planning and goals can yield a plethora of valuable insights for the target organization, also known as the _**“client,”**_ regarding security vulnerabilities that require attention. It is imperative for the client to carefully review these findings, prioritize them, and take decisive action to enhance their security posture.

While it is essential for security measures to be robust, they must also allow the system to function effectively and support the organization’s business processes; however, there are instances where a client may opt not to address certain issues. This decision could be influenced by factors such as the perceived severity of the problem or the cost associated with implementing a solution compared to the potential loss resulting from the vulnerability.

In cases where action is necessary, the primary challenge lies in ensuring that any modifications to security controls do not compromise the system’s usability and availability. Balancing security and convenience can be a delicate task, as enhancing security often comes at the expense of user convenience. This trade-off is particularly evident in authentication mechanisms, where transitioning from passwords to smart cards to biometrics increases security but may also lead to longer authentication times and user frustrations.

Ethical Hackers and the CIA Triad

Ethical hackers play a crucial role in assessing the fundamental principles of information security. These principles are often illustrated through the CIA triad, which stands for _**confidentiality, integrity,**_ and _**availability.**_

Confidentiality involves ensuring that only authorized individuals can access protected data. Integrity focuses on ensuring that only authorized individuals can make changes to protected data. Availability, on the other hand, ensures that information and the resources needed to manage it are readily accessible to authorized individuals.

Ethical hackers are responsible for evaluating and maintaining the security of these key components to safeguard sensitive information and prevent unauthorized access or modifications. By upholding the principles of the CIA triad, ethical hackers help organizations enhance their overall security architectures to protect against threats.
