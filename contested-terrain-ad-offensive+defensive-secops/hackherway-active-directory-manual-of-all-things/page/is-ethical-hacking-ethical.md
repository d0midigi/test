# Is Ethical Hacking Ethical

Is Ethical Hacking Ethical?

This section examines the moral considerations surrounding ethical hacking and examines the potential issues inherent of this profession. Ethical hacking has sparked debate in recent times, leading to unresolved questions about the genuine motives of those who practice it. Additionally, this section contemplates future research directions that could contribute to maintaining the integrity of ethical hacking.

Introduction

Grasping the true intentions of the general public is challenging, and it becomes even more complex when trying to discern the motives of each ethical hacker accessing vulnerable systems or networks. As technology continues to advance, we encounter tools that, while beneficial to the public, can cause significant controversy if misused, infringing on our fundamental rights to privacy, respect, and free will. The media frequently highlights various cybercrimes and studies indicate that nearly 90% of attacks originate from within organizations, raising concerns about the ease with which insiders can launch attacks. Has ethical hacking emerged as a solution to these problems, or has it introduced new challenges?

Education and Training in Ethical Hacking

The issue of teaching students to hack remains a significant concern today. Course leaders believe they are educating students to enhance security measures, but understanding the true intentions of students is challenging, making the justification for ethical hacking a topic of concern and debate. If a student uses their hacking knowledge to commit crimes, it raises societal questions about why they were taught to hack in the first place; however, blaming course leaders for allowing students to learn hacking is not straightforward, or even reasonable.

For instance, when cars are manufactured, they undergo crash testing to identify areas for improvement and ensure reliability. If companies did not test these issues, would it be the manufacturer’s fault if a car crash occurred? Similarly, teaching students to hack provides them with comprehensive knowledge of how to infiltrate computer systems, often with the guidance of university teachers and professors. The potential threats posed by these students are significant. Given the current mindset of some students, it is easy to imagine the kinds of threats they could pose, from committing violent acts to initiating terroristic plots. Universities, by teaching hacking, may inadvertently contribute to network damage, akin to giving a burglar a crowbar to break into houses.

A problem with teaching undergraduate students using this approach is that the instructor is effectively providing them with a loaded gun. Once students acquire new skills, they may use them for good or bad intentions. Certain policies are not being applied at universities to address students conducting malicious acts; however, these issues can be mitigated by implementing security checks for individuals enrolled in course like ethical hacking. Measures such as criminal background checks, professional certification requirements, and student interviews could potentially filter out students with malevolent intentions.

With numerous training courses available worldwide, understanding the reasons behind students’ interests in these courses is challenging. Some individuals may have a long-standing interest in security and aim to enhance their resumes for better job prospects and higher salaries. It is undeniable that ethical hackers are well-compensated. To some extent, ethical hacking is indeed ethical. Without such measures, we would need to manually ensure our systems’ safety; therefore, ethical hacking can ensure the security of our systems if conducted ethically.

Trusting the Potential Enemy

No two individuals in this world are the same in terms of appearance, shape, size, or mental state. Consequently, predicting the actions of any one person is challenging. To address security issues, companies might need to hire two completely different individuals to run tests, ensuring that no single person has total control over any one system. The need for secure information is crucial and may play a significant role in ethical hacking. People often seek to understand certain aspects of themselves or society, which can lead to significant issues regarding who can access and view this information.

Hacking for any gain, whether financial or personal, is wrong. For example, an ethical hacker working on a major project for a financial company to identify security flaws might later breach their contract out of curiosity or spite and sell their knowledge to criminals. This scenario highlights one of the many challenges ethical hacking faces today.

Ethics and religion are distinct, but both influence perceptions of right and wrong. For instance, Christinas and Muslims generally view adultery as a major sin; however, the urge to commit a wrongful act can still prevail despite ethical or religious teachings. This concept explains how different people have varying perceptions of right and wrong based on their religion, culture, or society. Hackers may know that gaining unauthorized access to systems is wrong, but they might still do it for pleasure or other reasons.

As technology advances, the digitization of data and business transactions is rapidly becoming the norm, ushering us into the next generation. For instance, eBay, a global auction site, encourages businesses to sell their goods, providing an auctioning platform from the comfort of home; however, ethical hackers may exploit their skills to avoid paying for items they purchase, using the knowledge to benefit themselves without getting caught. This duality means that ethical hackers can sometimes act as ‘white hats,’ by day and ‘black hats’ when it suits them best.

Unfortunately, some skilled professionals misuse their abilities to harm society. They exploit vulnerabilities in company systems, launch attacks, create and distribute malware, and find ways to avoid paying for desired services.

Corruption is a significant issue in ethical hacking, raising questions about trust. An ethical hacker might perform their job well, but their true intentions can be questionable. If an ethical hacker is corrupt, it could imply that the company is also corrupt if they deny any security lapses identified by the ethical hacker. If a company gets hacked after an ethical hacker’s assessment, the company might blame the security testers. While this scenario may seem extreme, it highlights the potential risks involved.

Risk Management

Ethical hackers are well-compensated professionals with legitimate access and status. They play a crucial role in minimizing risks by identifying both benefits and flaws, helping senior company directors decide whether certain activities should be undertaken. By exploring vulnerabilities in advance, ethical hackers can reduce potential risks. Companies often conduct penetration tests to determine their vulnerability to attacks. Identifying vulnerabilities not only benefits the company but also reduces the risk of attacks; however, ethical hackers typically have only five days to perform these tests. If vulnerabilities are overlooked, and an ethical hacker fails to deliver accurate results, assuming the system is safe, who is liable if a malicious hacker breaches the system?

An IBM journal on ethical hacking notes:<br>

People operate the client’s computers and networks, and people make mistakes. The longer it has been since the security testing was performed, the less can be reliably said about the state of a client’s actual security. A portion of the final report includes recommendations for steps the client should continue to follow to reduce the impact of these mistakes in the future.”_

Granting special privileges to users who then return with inaccurate information, as Palmer describes, makes us question the difference between this approach and using standard security software.

Deeper analysis shows that properly programming systems from the start can enhance security. The main concern is the cost of managing and administering these solutions. The issue of self-improvement also arises: should the company or the ethical hackers be responsible for increasing their knowledge? This knowledge could potentially be used to launch attacks from different parts of the world under the guise of ethical hacking.

Another perspective is whether legitimate ethical hackers, who aim to address security issues, should be allowed access to certain information and security barriers. To do their job effectively, they need some leeway and access to specific tools. The case of Randal Schwartz, who was sentenced for merely doing his job, highlights the necessity of using tools without question to identify security vulnerabilities.

Ethical hackers can identify problems, but to what extent? They might not detect a common virus eating away at data, or they might overlook it due to limited testing time. While hackers aim to bypass and deceive networks, ethical hackers might compromise the network by leaving it vulnerable until problems arise. This raises the issue of the “man on the inside,” suggesting that ethical hackers might inadvertently make it easier for malicious hackers to infiltrate.

Helping the Enemy

In our technological world, almost nothing is secure. Information is freely available to anyone determined enough to find it. CAPTCHA, a Turing test application, helps distinguish humans from computers, aiding in understanding and preventing attacks. By identifying human criminals and letting computers do their job, we can better manage security issues.

Many tools are available to help ethical hackers perform their tasks effectively. For example, NMap, which finds open ports, is freely available for anyone to download and use. Acunetix, a commercial package that tests for web application vulnerabilities, can also be accessed unethically by hackers using certain cracks found online. Both ethical and malicious hackers can use these tools; the difference lies in their intentions. Ethical hackers use them to benefit organizations by identifying security weaknesses, while malicious hackers use them for criminal purposes.

Google is a powerful search engine that can provide valuable and sometimes illegal information. This raises privacy concerns, as those who know how to use clever commands can obtain sensitive information. Is it ethical for Google to hold such information about individuals or companies? The answer is likely no, as it benefits hackers but harms their targets. Companies must ensure that employees do not send sensitive information over the internet.

Google can play a significant role in providing valuable and sometimes sensitive information, which is concerning for individuals and companies with valuable data. For example, if someone books a parcel delivery online, a hacker could intercept the booking and impersonate the company to intercept the package. Clever searches on Google can reveal private video camera feeds, allowing criminals to plan crimes without field research.

Ethical hackers could track the daily activities of a petrol station, for instance, and as thieves, they could calculate the best times to commit a crime. The most important and widely obtainable information is passwords. A search for “Index of /” +password.txt” can reveal various passwords from databases, enabling hackers to commit crimes.

Google is a powerful tool that assists hackers significantly. Minimizing this problem is challenging, as it would require separate servers to store information, which is costly and time-consuming. Allowing individuals to access such information increases knowledge of potential threats, whether they are terrorists or criminals, thus aiding in committing crimes. This raises the issue of whether Google can be blamed for providing hackers with such information.

Applying Ethical Hacking in Practice

When considering the real-life implications of ethical hacking, rather than just the theoretical aspects, we must seriously address the ethics of allowing ethical hackers access to high-caliber government systems, such as police databases or DVLA records. The potential for misuse is significant; a single memorized record not directly linked to the penetration tester at the time could be exploited, compromising the trust in the information.

Ethical hackers working in banks present another layer of controversy. They have access to valuable data, from student accounts to those of high-level executives. The temptation to steal or memorize account details could be substantial. With the prevalence of online fraud, tracking down ethical hackers and assigning blame becomes problematic. Even if an ethical hacker did not commit the crime, their access to accounts could implicate them, especially in environments prone to fraud. This raises important questions: if an ethical hacker is tasked with checking vulnerabilities in banking systems and accounts are hacked shortly after, who is to blame? This scenario undoubtedly raises concerns.

Now, consider a residential care home where systems are accessed for administrative and safety measures. Residents, whether in private homes, large public buildings, or care homes, are entitled to a certain level of privacy. Most security weaknesses stem from human error rather than computer failures, but computer failure rates can be between 10-15%. During such failures, a hacker could exploit the system. In this context, each resident would have a number, and their daily routines and whereabouts could be easily accessed via the network. No one would be comfortable knowing that network administrators could determine when they were showering, using the bathroom, or getting ready for bed. This scenario highlights another potential ethical issue: could such a system potentially violate the privacy of residents?

Furthermore, the ethical implications extend beyond just privacy concerns. Allowing ethical hackers access to sensitive systems could inadvertently provide them with the knowledge and tools to exploit these systems later. This dual role of ethical hackers—protectors by day and potential threats by night—complicates the trust dynamic. The ethical hacker’s intent and the potential for misuse must be carefully considered, especially in environments where sensitive information is at stake.

In conclusion, while ethical hackers play a crucial role in identifying and mitigating security vulnerabilities, the potential for misuse and the ethical implications of their access to sensitive systems cannot be overlooked. It is essential to establish stringent guidelines and oversight to ensure that the benefits of ethical hacking are realized without compromising the trust and security of the systems they are meant to protect.
