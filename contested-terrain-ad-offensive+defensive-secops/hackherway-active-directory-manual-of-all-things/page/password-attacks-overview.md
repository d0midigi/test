# Password Attacks Overview

### Password Attacks Overview

Passwords are the access verification tool of choice for most people, so figuring out a target’s password is an attractive proposition for a malicious hacker. This can be done using a few different methods. Often, people keep copies of their passwords on pieces of paper or sticky notes around or on their desks and monitors. An adversary can either find the passwords themselves or pay someone on the inside to retrieve them.

An adversary may also try to intercept network transmissions to grab passwords that are not encrypted and ones that are sent in cleartext across the network. Social engineering can also be used to convince the target to input their password to solve a seemingly “important” problem. In other cases, the adversary can simply brute-force or guess the user’s password, particularly if they use a default password or one that is easy to remember such as “1234567.”

Adversaries also often use brute-force methods to guess passwords. A brute-force password hack uses basic information about the individual or their job title to try and guess their password. For example, their name, birthdate, anniversary, or other identifying details but easy-to-discover details can be used in various combinations to decipher their password.

Information that users put on social media can also be leveraged in a brute-force password hack. What the individual does for fun, specific hobbies, names of pets, or names of spouses or children are sometimes used to form passwords, making them relatively easy to guess for brute-force password hack attacks.

An adversary can also use a dictionary attack to ascertain a user’s password. A dictionary attack is a technique that uses common words and phrases, such as those listed in a dictionary, to try and guess the target’s password.

One effective method of preventing brute-force and dictionary password attacks is to set up a lockout policy. This locks out access to devices, websites, or applications automatically after a certain number of failed attempts. With a lockout policy, the adversary only has a few tries before they get banned from access. If you have a lockout policy in place already and discover that your account has been locked out because of too many login attempts, it is wise to change your password.

If an adversary systematically uses a brute-force or dictionary attack to guess your password, they may take note of the passwords that did not work. For example, if your password is your last name followed by your year of birth and the adversary tries your birth year before your last name on the final attempt, they may get it right on the next try.
