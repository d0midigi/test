**Case Studies of Cyberattacks**

The following are just a few examples of this trend:

**Latitude Financial (March 2023)**

* **Company:** Latitude Financial, a Melbourne-based company providing personal loans and credit cards in Australia and New Zealand.
* **Attack:** More than 14 million records were compromised, including nearly 8 million driver’s licenses, 53,000 passport identification numbers, and numerous financial statements were leaked. An additional 6 million records dating back to “at least 2005” were also compromised.
* **Outcome:** The original disclosure that only 300,000 people were affected led to public scrutiny and potential loss of trust among customers. This incident highlights the importance of accurate and timely communication in the aftermath of a data breach.

**Unprotected Real Estate Wealth Network (December 2023)**

* **Company:** Real Estate Wealth Network, based in New York, NY, USA.
* **Attack:** Over 1.5 billion records containing property ownership data were exposed. The data included information on property owners, sellers, investors, and internal user logging data, potentially involving numerous celebrities.
* **Outcome:** The exposure of such extensive data raises concerns about privacy and the potential misuse of sensitive information.

**TuneFab (September 2023)**

* **Company:** TuneFab, a platform converting music from popular streaming services to other formats.
* **Attack:** More than 151 million data records were exposed, including users’ IP addresses, user areas, user IDs, emails, and device information.
* **Outcome:** The swift action by TuneFab in fixing the misconfiguration within 24 hours mitigated the impact of the breach.

**Dori Media Group (December 2023)**

* **Company:** Dori Media Group, an international group of media companies operating in Israel, Switzerland, Argentina, Spain, and Singapore.
* **Attack:** Allegedly, more than 100 terabytes of data were exfiltrated, with threats to leak the data.
* **Outcome:** The scale of the data exfiltration underscores the severity of the breach and the potential for significant harm if the leaked data is misused.

**Recognizing Trouble When It Happens**

Ethical hackers, network administrators, engineers, and security professionals must be able to recognize when an attack is underway or when one is imminent. It may seem like it should be easy enough to recognize an attack as it is happening right in front of you – but only for the very “noisy” or overwhelming attacks such as Denial of Service (DoS) attacks. Many attackers fly under the radar and go unnoticed by security devices and security staff. By knowing how different types of cyberattacks work, you can properly recognize them and swiftly move to stop them.

You also need to know when an attack may be around the corner. If you educate network staff on attacker Techniques, Tactics, and Procedures (TTPs), and they see a ping sweep followed a day later by a port scan, they know that their systems may soon be under attack. Many activities exhibit certain indicators of compromise (IOCs) that lead up to different types of cyberattacks, so having a thorough and full understanding of various unique and individual attack types can help a company to better protect its infrastructure.

**Using Hacking Tools Ethically**

Hacking tools are just software tools that carry out some specific types of procedures to achieve a desired result. These tools can be used for good (defensive) purposes or for bad (offensive) purposes. The good and the bad guys use the same exact toolsets; the only real difference is in the intent when operating these tools. It is imperative for ethical hackers and security professionals to understand how to use these tools and how cyberattacks are carried out if they are going to be of any use to their customers and to the industry.

**3. The Ethical Hacking Process**

To protect themselves, organizations may want or have the need to understand the impact and ability of a malicious attacker. In this case, they may employ an ethical hacker, also known as a penetration tester, red teamer, or offensive security specialist, to simulate an attack against their environment(s). The techniques that ethical hackers employ are designed to emulate those of real attackers without causing damage or interruption; they enable organizations to better protect themselves against cyberattacks. But customers and aspiring hackers need to fully understand how this process works.

**Defining Penetration Testing vs. Vulnerability Assessments**

Before describing the process of ethical hacking and penetration testing, we first must discuss the differences between penetration testing and vulnerability assessments. These activities have different goals, but are often confused with one another depending on who you may talk to.

During a vulnerability assessment, some type of automated scanning product is used to probe the ports and services using a range of networked IP addresses. Most of these products can also test for the type of operating system and application software running and the versions, patch levels, user accounts, and services that are also running. These findings are then matched up with correlating vulnerabilities in the product’s database. The end result is a large pile of data that basically states, “Here is a list of your vulnerabilities and here is a list of the things you need to do to fix them.”

The problem with most vulnerability scans is, although they may indicate the severity of a vulnerability, they rarely indicate its impact. This is where ethical hacking and penetration testing comes into play. Vulnerability scanning allows you to identify a piece of software as being vulnerable to a specific exploit; a penetration test takes this further by exploiting vulnerabilities and, for example, accessing sensitive information. Most vulnerability scanners indicate what might be deemed as vulnerable based on versioning and some other invasive checks, but a penetration test indicates whether the vulnerability scanner finding is real or a false positive.

When penetration testers attack, their ultimate goal is usually to break into a system and hop from system to system until they “pwn” the domain or environment. Unlike a vulnerability assessment, a penetration test does not stop with the identification of a possible vulnerability. Ethical hackers leverage identified vulnerabilities until they own the domain or environment. Being “pwned” means either having root privileges on the most critical Unix or Linux system or owning the domain administrator account that can access and control all of the resources on a network. Ethical hackers do this to show the customer (company or client) what an actual malicious attacker can do under the circumstances and the network’s current security posture.

**The Ethical Hacker Penetration Process**

Once network administrators, engineers, and security professionals understand how malicious attackers work, they can emulate their activities to carry out a useful penetration test. But why would anyone want to emulate a real-world simulation of an oncoming, and aggressive cyberattack? Because this is the only way to truly test an environment’s security stance – if you’re going to be successful in this field, you must know how it will react when a real cyberattack is being carried out.
