# Ransomware Overview

### Ransomware Overview

With ransomware, the victim’s system and its data are held hostage until the victim agrees to pay a ransom to the adversary. After the payment has been sent (usually in the form of a Bitcoin (BTC)), the attacker either i) provides instructions regarding how the target can regain control of their computer and its data through the use of a decryptor, or ii) takes off with the victim’s money leaving the victim penniless and data-less. The name ransomware is appropriate because the malware that is locking the victim’s contents demands ransom.

In a ransomware attack, the target downloads the malicious software either from a website or from within an email attachment. The malware is then written to exploit vulnerabilities that have not been addressed by either the system’s manufacturer or the IT team. The ransomware then encrypts the target’s workstation. At times, ransomware can be used to attack multiple parties by denying access to either several computers or a centralized server essential to business operations.

Affecting multiple computers is often accomplished by not initiating system captivation until days or even weeks after the malware’s initial penetration. The malware can send AUTORUN files that go from one system to another via the internal network or Universal Serial Bus (USB) drives that connect to multiple computers. Then, when the attacker initiates the encryption, it works on all the infected systems simultaneously.

In some cases, ransomware authors design code to evade traditional antivirus software. It is, therefore, important for users to remain vigilant regarding which sites they visit, and which links they click. You can also prevent many ransomware attacks by using a next-generation firewall (NGFW) that can perform deep data packet inspections using artificial intelligence (AI) that looks for the characteristics of ransomware.
