# Chapter 35 - Identity-Driven Mission Impact, Access Disruption, and Controlled Proof of Consequence

### Abstract

Identity compromise becomes operationally significant when control of authentication, authorization, policy, trust, credentials, applications, or recovery systems can produce measurable mission effect. This chapter examines how identity authority can affect confidentiality, integrity, availability, administrative access, mission applications, security tooling, cloud resources, PKI, federation, and recovery capability across federal and DoD environments. Offensive analysis focuses on translating control-plane compromise into defensible proof of consequence without unnecessary disruption, including controlled demonstrations of unauthorized access, privilege alteration, authentication dependency, policy influence, trust manipulation, and recovery exposure. Defensive analysis emphasizes blast-radius reduction, protected administrative access, identity-service resilience, independent recovery, change detection, continuity planning, containment, and rapid restoration of trusted authority. Particular attention is given to distinguishing technical capability from mission effect and to determining when evidence is sufficient without executing the most damaging action available. The chapter establishes the governing principle for offensive identity operations: the objective is not maximum control or destruction, but credible proof of what compromised identity authority could enable and the defensive engineering required to prevent that consequence.
