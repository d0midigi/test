The short answer to this is, if you don't hack your own systems, who will? whether you are an aspiring ethical hacker, red or blue teamer, an information security professional, or someone who is is in charge of cybersecurity policies or oversee defensive tactics and  implementations, you are responsible for evaluating just how well an information system or application can withstand real-world cyberattacks and for making informed decisions about its risks and resilience.

The central question is not simply *"Is this system secure?"* but rather *"If this system is exposed to a determined an motivated attacker, how long an it endure sustained pressure before it falls?"* For mature, long-running systems, you may have historical data or prior assessments to guide this judgment. for new, rapidly evolving, or poorly understood systems, however, you have little or no empirical basis for confidence. In these cases, the responsibility for validating security assumptions falls squarely on you and your security team.

This is why you should be hacking; to actively test how your systems behave under realistic attack conditions and to observe how long they can resist before weaknesses emerge. even if you never discover  sophisticated, novel exploit, you can and should apply systematic,  methodical tests using well-known techniques and basic attack patterns. Time and time again organizations discover that a surprisingly large proportion of systems fail when subjected only to fundamental checks such as weak password probing, misconfiguration testing of security controls, or trivial privilege escalation attempts.

By deliberately attacking your own infrastructure in a controlled, ethical manner, you replace guesswork with raw evidence. You gain concrete data about where your defenses hold best, where they collapse quickly, and where small changes yield significant improvements of existing security architecture.

Ultimately, hacking your own systems is not an optional exercise in futility or a niche specialization; it is a core security practice that enables you to better understand your environment, prioritize fixes, and build defenses that are defined by how attackers actually operate in the real-word.
