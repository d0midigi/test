**Introduction**



Information security policies, procedures, and guidelines (PPGs) play a significant role in helping us form and sustain our social, governmental, and corporate organizations. In this chapter, we will begin by defining the role of policy companions, including standards, guidelines, and procedures and the role they play in supporting, implementing, and maintaining policy compliance. We will then break policy down into its essential components and focus on the elements that are required to make the document an official, living document usable in the most effective and efficient way possible.



Some of the necessary elements of information security policies are mandated by regulatory requirements such as the Gramm-Leach Bliley Act (GLBA) and the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA). These two federal laws require specific information be contained in an organization's information security policy, such as the date the policy was written and revised, a schedule for future review and revision, and a statement with consequences of violation(s).



Other elements that make a security policy document usable in the most effective and efficient way possible include the objectives of the policy, a statement of purpose, a statement of exceptions, and the policy statements themselves.



This chapter provides a detailed explanation of each of the recommended elements of an information security policy, though you should find these same elements in any policy document used for any other purpose. We will also explore how these elements fit together and how they rely on and support each other while also supporting the document as a whole.



**Defining Policy Companions: Policies, Procedures, Guidelines (PPGs), and Standards**



Policies are important, but alone, they do not accomplish very much. Policies need support - companion documents that support and give the overall policy meaningful applications, a foundation from which they are conceived, and suggestions for how to best accomplish tasks associated with meeting their expectations.



Three kinds of documents are considered "policy implementation" documents, or companion documents to policy. In this section, we will explore the uses of these three kinds of documents: *policies, procedures,* *and guidelines (PPGs),* as well as *standards.* Each play a significant role in turning policies into repeatable actions that are based on sound ideas and judgment.



Before we move on, it is first imperative that you understand that with creating, maintaining, publishing, and controlling an information security policy has its own set of troublesome pitfalls. Below, we warn you about the most troublesome of these pitfalls. Many organizations make the mistake of combining policies with the three other companion documents into one big, unruly document no one in their right mind would want to read in its entirety. Many problems have the potential to arise from this mistake, including, but not limited to:



* Difficulty of implementation: With all of these documents combined into one massive document, how do I communicate to each different department just what it needs to know to implement the policy?
* Difficult of management: How do I maintain control over such a large document? The task becomes overwhelming pushing people away from completing the responsibility of keeping the policy in tip top shape.
* Difficulty in updating: Since standards, guidelines, and procedures change far more often than do policies, updating this whale of a document will be far more difficult than if these elements were properly treated as separate. Also, since C-Suite and Board-Level Directors in many business sectors must approve and sign off on these policies before they are enforced enterprise-wide, and if we combine all of these documents into one large document, every change to a procedure, guideline, or standard will require the Board's review for approval. This will become very costly and time-consuming, especially cumbersome for everyone involved.



**Standards**



***Standards*** dictate specific minimum requirements within information security policies. THe most easily understood standard, for our time and purpose, is the password standard. A password standard dictates how an acceptable password is constructed. It might read something like this:



Password must meet the following standard(s):

* Minimum of 14 upper- and lowercase alphanumeric characters.
* Must include at least one special character (e.g., !\&#).
* Must be changed every 30 days.
* Password history of 24 previous passwords will be used to ensure the restricting of password reuse.



Our password policy, on the other hand, will read like this:

* All users must have a unique user ID and password that conforms to the company password standard.
* Users must not share their password with anyone regardless of title or position.
* Passwords must not be stored in written or any readable form.
* If a password is suspected as compromised, it must be reported immediately to the help desk so that a new password can be requested and issued.



From this example, which do you think might change more often, the standard or the policy? Which is more general? Clearly, the policy represents expectation that are not subject to changes in technology, management, or foreseeable circumstances. The standard, however, is very specific to the current industry best practice and the technology platform in use.



**Guidelines**



While standards are very definite and required, ***guidelines*** are best thought of as suggestions for the best way to accomplish a certain task. In addition to using softer language, guidelines are more likely to change more often than any other element in this family of documents. While our password standard is very specific, we can create a supporting guideline to help users conform to our standard. It might read like this:



A good way to create a strong password is to think of a phrase, song title, or other group of words that is easy for you to remember (yet hard for others to guess) and to remember and convert it like this:

* The phrase *Up and At 'em at 7!* can be converted into a strong password appearing like this: 7!@UpatM\&.
* You can create many passwords from this one phrase by changing the number, moving the symbols or changing the punctuation mark to another symbol. <--this is still considered a form of password reuse.



Guidelines offer suggestions for the best way to accomplish a task, as this helpful hint on strong password creation helps computer users to create easy-to-remember, yet strong passwords.



**Procedures**



A ***procedure*** provides a method by which a policy is accomplished; the instructions necessary to carry out a policy statement. Procedures focus on actions or steps and there is usually a starting point and an ending point to that action. In keeping with our other examples, let's demonstrate a procedure for changing a user password:



REPLACE FOR MFA/BIOMETRICS, THIS PASSWORD SHIT IS OUTDATED



There isn't much grey area in explaining a procedure. Just remember it as a series of steps to complete a task.



**Developing a Policy Style and Format**



Make sure you carefully consider the style and organization of your information security policies and companion documents. Style is critical. How the document flows and the words you use will make all the difference as to how the policy is followed and interpreted. You need to know your intended audience and write in a way that is relevant and understandable. For example, one needs to be articulate in the art of explaining technical concepts to non-technical audiences in a way that they can understand (e.g., explaining a network routing policy to board members who might not be all that technically savvy). For example, consider the difference in the vocabulary you would use if you are writing a policy to be read by employees of a multinational organization for whom English may be a second language versus a policy for high school and college students working as summer camp counselors.



**Plan Before You Write**



Organize before you begin writing! It is important to first decide how many sections and subsections you will require before you put pen to paper. By designing a template that allows you the flexiblitliy of changing your policy at any moment's time, you will save yourself a considerable amount of time not to mention aggravation.



There are two schools of thought with regard to formatting policies. The first is to write each plicy as a discrete individual document. The second is to write one large policy document with multiple sections. We prefer to write our policies and treat them as individual documents. We like this approach for two reasons specifically: (1) each policy document can be short, clean, and crisp, and (2) each policy can be specifically targeted to its intended audience. Of course, of purposes of accessibility, policy documents need to be stored together. This can be done by using a binder, a shared folder, or on your company intranet.

As much as possible, our goal is to keep policy documents short and sweet; generally fewer than two pages max. We begin each policy with a clear heading that includes the name of the policy, the security domain it belongs to, and housekeeping such as date, version, and approval. The body of the policy includes the following key elements, that **must be included** in order for the policy to be considered accurate and complete:

* Objectives

**Statement of Authority (Delegation of Authority)**

A federal statement of authority policy (often referred to as a Statement of Organization, Functions, and Delegations of Authority) is a formal directive used by government agencies to legally delegate powers, assign functional responsibilities, and designate which officials can bind the agency or sign specific documents.

The ***statement of authority***, also commonly referred to as a ***Delegation of Authority***,  is *not* found in each individual policy document but rather serves as a preface to a group of policies and to your information security program. The statement of authority fucntions as a general introduction  to the information security policies, where the thought process behind the actual policies is presented to the reader, and it explains what motivated the company to draft these documents. It often sets forth the regulatory compliance responsibilities that the company has - often by listing which federal regulations such as GLBA, HIPAA, or Sarbanes-Oxley (SOX) pertain to the organization.



More than that, it is also a statement of culture, one that attmepts to define for the reader the core values form a security policy standpoint to ensure that these values can coexist with a realistic and strong security strategy.



The statement of authority also usually attempts to "recruit" readers and show them what is expected of them as employees.


To achieve this, the statement of authority typically uses such language as "the company has identified the rules set forth in this document as a requirement for all employees to conform to in order to defend both the company and protect the confidential data with which it is entrusted against attacks" and "this document was constructed to provide assistance, guidance, and protection to our employees as it pertains to their professional responsibilities to both use and safeguard the company's confidential data." These statements are juxtaposed to both include the employees in a caring, supportive, and responsible professional structure and to explicitly outline for the employees' sake that, as member of this professional entity, they are unequivocally and directly responsible for the safeguarding of the confidential data that crosses their desks in the course of their normal employment by the company.

The main functions of authority delegation must address the following:

* Administrative process of the Organization must operate smoothly while efficiency and effectiveness experience optimization.
* The organization needs to grant necessary authority to staff members who demonstrate competence to carry out their duties properly.
* Authority should be delegated to users who both possess essential information and the best qualifications to achieve sound decisio-making and outputs.
The Organization needs to keep strong systems for internal control to defend fundamental integrities.
The Organization must protect its staff members and volunteers who execute authorized decision-making in their assigned duty boundary limits.
* Through an established mechanism, the CEO of the organization must ensure that executive-level staff, along with senior volunteers and staff, possess the necessary skills to make decisions and enter into commitments representing the company, leading to more efficient administrative processes.

**In Practice:** Example of a Statement of Authority



The structure template below outlines a real-world federal administrative policy, mirroring formats frequently published in the Federal Register.

Policy Template Structure

1. Purpose
This policy establishes the delegation of authority and functional responsibilities within [Insert Federal Agency/Department Name]. It ensures accountability, maintains internal controls, and defines which officers are legally authorized to sign contracts, issue regulations, and approve agency programs on behalf of the [Agency Head Title, e.g., Secretary or Administrator].

2. Delegation of Authority

* Primary Authority: The [Agency Head] retains all statutory and regulatory authority vested by Congress.

* Redelegation: The [Agency Head] delegates specific administrative and programmatic functions to the [Sub-Agency Role, e.g., Chief Operating Officer or Assistant Secretary for Administration].

* Limitations: The delegated authorities must be exercised in compliance with all applicable federal statutes, Office of Management and Budget (OMB) circulars, and Departmental guidelines. Redelegation may be subject to specific grade/level restrictions.

3. Authorized Functions & Responsibilities
The [Delegated Official] is authorized to perform the following functions within their scope:

* Human Resources: Admnister personnel management, labor relations, and equal employment opportunity (EEO) programs.

* Acquisitions & Finance: Approve contracts, execute financial management, and oversee property/asset management.

* Information Technology: Manage IT portfolios and direct cybersecurity and information quality compliance.

4. Redelegation Procedures
Authority delegated under this policy may be redelegated in writing. All redelegations must be submitted to the agency's [Office of the Executive Secretariat/Policy Office] and must explicitly identify the authorized officiant, the scope of the delegated authority, and any financial or administrative limitations.

5. Supersession & Effective Date
This policy supersedes all previous Statements of Organization, Functions, and Delegations of Authority.

* Effective Date: [Insert Date]
