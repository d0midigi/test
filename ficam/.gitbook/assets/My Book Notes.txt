This layout will further distinguish the book from generic Active Directory texts. Instead of saying “Here’s what everyone uses,” it’s saying “Here’s the guidance hierarchy that federal agencies, defense organizations, and mission environments actually rely on.” This makes the content more authentic for the audience I’m targeting.
Federal and military entities utilize a tiered set of security baselines. The foundation for all federal mandated security is the NIST SP 800-53 control catalog, which mandates High, Moderate, and Low baselines. Civilian agencies, on the other hand, use CISA’s SCuBA framework, while DoD uses strict DISA STIG and SRGs for hardware/software hardening.
The DoD and Federal Government mandate several distinct compliance and security control configuration baselines:
1.	Cloud & Enterprise Baselines
•	FedRAMP: A term used for the mandatory baseline combined with additional, specialized security overlays, required by the DoD or Intelligence Community (IC). FedRAMP is the universal baseline for commercial Cloud Service Providers (CSPs). It classifies data by potential impact level:
o	Low/Moderate: Used for public-facing or internal agency data.
o	High: For the government’s most sensitive, unclassified data

•	DoD Cloud Computing Security Requirements Guide (SRG): Builds on FedRAMP by layering defense-specific controls and mandates.
o	Impact Levels (IL): DoD cloud deployments span from L2 (Public) to IL4/IL5 (Controlled Unclassified Information (CUI) and Mission-Critical Data) up to IL6 (Secret).

2.	Device & Configuration Baselines
o	Security Technical Implementation Guides (STIGs): The definitive cybersecurity standard used by the DoD, DISA, and the Intelligence Community to secure network devices, software, and operating systems.
o	United States Government Configuration Baseline (USGCB): A federal, government-wide initiative designed to standardize security configuration settings on IT products across civilian agencies.
o	CISA Secure Configuration Baselines: Agency-issued guidelines tailored specifically to cloud-based business applications (like Microsoft 365) to protect Federal Civilian Executive Branch (FCEB) assets.

3.	Overarching Architectural Frameworks
o	Risk Management Framework (RMF): The mandatory process for managing cybersecurity risk across the DoD, IAW NIST. It is mandated by DoDI 8510.01.
o	Zero Trust Architecture (ZTA): Both the DoD and CISA have migrated to Zero Trust, assuming no implicit trust is granted to any user or asset regardless of location. These principles are enforced across DoD networks as outlined in the DoD Zero Trust Reference Architecture documentation.

4.	Military Branches: Navy, Marine Corps, Army, Air Force, and Space Force
While individual military branches must strictly comply with overarching DoD requirements (like DISA STIGs, and RMF), each branch overlays its own tailored security baselines to address their unique operational environments – whether on land, at sea, or in the air.

4.1	Department of the Navy (USN) & United States Marine Corps (USMC)
The Navy and Marine Corps focus heavily on securing afloat networks (ships and submarines) operating with limited or intermittent bandwidth, shifting away from “point-in-time” compliance toward a continuous, consistent security posture.
o	“Cyber Ready” & Cyber Currency: The Department of Navy (DON) Chief Information Officer (CIO) Cyber Ready initiate replaces traditional, bureaucratic static assessments. Programs must maintain “Cyber Currency” through continuous automated penetration testing and Breach and Adversarial Security Assessments (BAS).
o	Afloat Cyber Baselines (FRD 300): Managed by NAVWAR, this framework ensures that tactical shipboard networks – such as the Consolidated Afloat Networks and Enterprise Services (CANES) – strictly comply with naval cybersecurity technical authority standards before production deployments.
o	Flank Speed & Flank Speed Mercury: The Navy’s baseline cloud enterprise environment, which enforces a Zero Trust architecture across unclassified and classified networks.

4.2	Department of the Air Force (USAF & USSF)
The Air Force and Space Force prioritize rapid software deployment at the tactical edge (such as in-flight fighter jets or orbiting satellites) and are aggressively modernizing legacy infrastructure.
o	Platform One: The Air Force’s premier, managed DevSecOps enterprise team. It provides pre-hardened Kubernetes and containerized security baselines, allowing software platforms to achieve continuous Authority to Operate (cATO).
o	Network of the Future Strategy: Issued by the DAF CIO, this framework sets the security baseline standard for integrating commercial encrypted networking technologies into military digital infrastructure.
o	DAFGM 2024-32 (Control Systems): A specialized baseline directive that tailor’s cybersecurity specifically to Operational Technologies (OT) and civil engineering infrastructure, prioritizing availability and integrity over standard IT confidentiality.

4.3	Department of the Army (USA)
The Army emphasizes securing tactical communications networks for soldiers on the move and standardizing software factories.
o	Army Software Factory & DevSecOps Playbook: Establishes the secure baselines for building custom, cloud-native tools. It uses automated “security control gates” to continuously vet code before field deployments.
o	Unified Network Plan: The operational baseline designed to converge the Army’s disparate technical (battlefield) and enterprise (garrison) networks into a single, highly secure Zero Trust architecture.
Addressing Reader Audience
The audience is not just “federal.” There are multiple identity ecosystems with different authoritative guidance:
•	Federal Civilian Executive Branch (FCEB)
•	Department of Defense
•	Intelligence Community
•	Military Departments
•	Defense Contractors (through DFARS/CMMC where applicable)
Those communities all share NIST as a foundation, but they diverge significantly in implementation guidance.
I think I should change the philosophy of the book
Rather than saying: “These are the standards.”
I should say: “These are the applicable standards depending on the mission environment.”
This is a much more accurate representation of the realities.
