Chapter Abstract (merged, final)
Active Directory was designed to centralize identity, authentication, authorization, and administrative control across an enterprise. The same capabilities that make it efficient also concentrate agency and mission risk, because control of the directory's most trusted core equals control of the agency's enterprise authority to decide who or what can be absolutely trusted.

Chapter 1 establishes this book's governing argument: the correct unit of analysis is not solely the Active Directory domain itself, but the broader federal identity trust system in which the domain performs its functions. That system includes directory, certificate, and federation services; the Federal Public Key Infrastructure (FPKI); credential and identity-proofing mechanisms such as Personal Identity Verification (PIV) and Common Access Cards (CAC); authoritative attributes; synchronization and cloud service provider (CSP) services; Privileged Identity Management (PIM) and Privileged Access Management (PAM) solutions; mission partners; and the governance and mission assurance requirements that bind them together.

The chapter also explains why Active Directory remains deeply embedded in modern enterprise environments. It traces the evolution of enterprise directory services from Novell NetWare and Novell Directory Services (NDS) through the introduction and widespread adoption of Active Directory, showing how Windows integration, Kerberos authentication, Lightweight Directory Access Protocol (LDAP), Domain Name System (DNS), Group Policy, application compatibility, and backward-compatible administration contributed to Microsoft's dominance. That history provides the context for the legacy protocols, configuration debt, administrative dependencies, and hybrid identity relationships that continue to shape the modern attack surface.

The chapter explains why commercial Active Directory analysis, which typically stops where federal identity governance begins, is incomplete in federal and military environments. It identifies the practitioner audience the book serves, states the book's contribution as the integration of disciplines usually treated separately, and introduces the recurring analytical method and the Active Directory Identity Attack Lifecycle that structure the chapters that follow. It closes by framing the federal identity threat landscape that the subsequent chapters examine.

1.3 The Federal Identity Trust Problem
When Active Directory operates in a federal or defense environment, it operates inside a framework that predates it, outranks it, and defines what its identities are allowed to mean. That framework begins with mission, law, and policy — not with a Microsoft product. This section establishes the federal identity architecture through which every technical finding in this book must ultimately be read: the government-wide FICAM lens, the DoD ICAM lens, the entities the framework governs, the proofing and credentialing processes that create identity, the assurance levels that measure it, the authoritative attributes that feed it, and the trust chain that connects it to resource access.

1.3.1 Why Federal Identity Is Not a Microsoft Architecture Problem
A domain is a technical construct. A federal identity is a legal, policy, and assurance construct. Federal identity begins with mission, law, policy, assurance, identity proofing, credentialing, access governance, federation, and accountability — none of which is a Microsoft product. When an agency issues a PIV credential or the Department issues a CAC, the legitimacy of that credential does not originate in Active Directory. It originates in statutory authority, an approved identity proofing process, a sponsorship and approval chain, and an issuance process governed by federal standards. Active Directory performs the last step — mapping the credential to an account and accepting it during authentication. It does not create the identity, and it does not make the credential trustworthy.

The practical consequence cuts both ways. An assessment that treats federal identity as a Microsoft architecture problem will harden the directory and miss the decisions that give the directory its authority. A review that treats federal identity as a policy problem will produce governance documents that describe intent while the implemented environment does something else. The adversary does not read the policy. The adversary operates the implemented trust chain.

This is also an accountability question. When a mission is disrupted or an authorization fails, the federal framework demands evidence: who was proofed, who issued the credential, who approved the access, who performed the action, and which controls were in place. Active Directory generates and protects a significant share of that evidence — which is why compromise of the directory does not merely threaten access. It threatens the agency's ability to account for what happened in its own environment. Section 1.10 returns to this requirement in detail.

1.3.2 FICAM as the Government-Wide Identity Architecture Lens
FICAM — the Federal Identity, Credential, and Access Management program — is the United States government's enterprise approach to identity, credential, and access management. It is an architecture and governance lens, not a product, not an edition of Active Directory, and not a deployment model. It spans identity management, credential management, access management, federation, assurance, identity proofing, credential binding, attribute governance, Person Entities, Non-Person Entities, mission-partner trust, and identity lifecycle management.

FICAM's operational role in this book is translation. The lens gives the defender a way to convert a technical finding into a federal identity consequence. An overprivileged service account is, under this lens, an NPE whose credential management, attribute governance, and lifecycle controls have failed — with consequences for every system that relies on that identity. A misconfigured certificate template is a failure of credential issuance controls. A stale group membership is a failure of attribute governance and lifecycle synchronization. The lens does not change the technical finding. It changes what the finding means, what evidence it must produce, and which authority must respond.

For the practitioner, the discipline is to run every finding through the lens before it reaches a decision-maker. A finding that stops at "this account can modify that group" is an inventory observation. A finding that continues to "this identity's access exceeds what its lifecycle, sponsorship, and approval records justify, and the systems relying on it include mission systems X and Y" is a federal identity finding. The first is read by the directory team. The second is read by an authorizing official.

1.3.3 DoD ICAM as the Department-Specific Policy and Implementation Lens
DoD ICAM applies federal identity principles within the Department of Defense's mission, operational, enterprise, coalition, and mission-partner environments. It is the Department-specific strategy, architecture, policy, reference design, and implementation context — not an extension of FICAM software and not an edition of Active Directory.

The distinction that matters is scope and authority. FICAM is the government-wide frame; DoD ICAM is the department-specific frame. Both rest on the same principles — proofed identity, issued credentials, governed attributes, federated trust, and lifecycle accountability. They differ in implementation: credential portfolio (CAC versus PIV), identifiers (EDIPI), assurance requirements, the mission context of classified fabrics, coalition operations, and operational availability, and the governance authorities that enforce them.

This distinction carries a warning for the reader. The same technical finding translates into different evidence and authorization consequences depending on the lens. A civilian agency documents against FICAM, NIST, and OMB direction. A DoD component documents against DoD ICAM, DoDI 8510.01, DISA STIGs and SRGs, and the DoD Zero Trust Reference Architecture. A configuration that satisfies a civilian agency's FICAM-based requirements does not automatically satisfy DoD ICAM requirements, and the reverse is equally true. The practitioner must know which framework governs their environment, what evidence that framework demands, and where the two frames disagree about the meaning of the same condition.

1.3.4 Person Entities and Non-Person Entities
The federal identity framework distinguishes two classes of subject. Person Entities are human identities: employees, service members, contractors, mission partners, applicants, and beneficiaries. Non-Person Entities are everything else that acts within an information system: devices, applications, services, workloads, processes, scripts, bots, automation platforms, and certificate-enabled components. In Active Directory terms, the NPE population is the computer accounts, service accounts, managed service accounts, application identities, synchronization accounts, and security-tooling accounts that in many environments now outnumber human users.

Person Entities carry a lifecycle that is roughly understood across the government: proofing, sponsorship, credentialing, role changes, suspension, separation. The processes are imperfect, but they exist, and separation events trigger them. NPEs carry no such lifecycle by default. A service account has no departure date, no sponsor who leaves, no recertification reminder, and no natural event that causes anyone to ask whether it should still exist. An NPE with a static password, replication rights, and no documented owner is a governance failure that presents as a routine technical condition.

The directory does not distinguish between the two classes when enforcing authority. A person entity and an NPE are both security principals that authenticate, hold permissions, and appear in logs. That is exactly why the governance must differ. NPE governance requires an accountable owner, a documented mission function, unique identification, credential and key protection, a defined access scope, rotation and renewal, monitoring and attribution, and secure retirement. Treating an NPE as an unmanaged technical exception because it is not a human is how machine identities become the quietest and longest-lived paths in the attack surface.

1.3.5 Identity Proofing, Credential Issuance, and Credential Binding
Before an identity can authenticate, three processes must have occurred in sequence. Identity proofing establishes that the claimed identity corresponds to a real person, by collecting, validating, and verifying evidence before or during enrollment. Credential issuance creates the credential — the PIV card, the CAC, the certificate, the authenticator — through an approved authority after proofing, sponsorship, and approval requirements are satisfied. Credential binding securely associates that credential with the established identity record. Authentication, which happens later and repeatedly, determines whether the entity presenting the credential is the legitimate subscriber bound to it.

The distinction between proofing and authentication matters more in practice than in definition. Proofing establishes who the subject is. Authentication establishes whether the entity presenting the authenticator is that subject. An attacker does not need to defeat the proofing process if the binding is weak. A credential that is valid, properly issued, and correctly proofed can be associated with the wrong identity through a failed or manipulated binding — and every subsequent authentication with that credential authenticates the wrong person through mechanisms the directory is designed to honor.

In Active Directory terms, the binding points are the certificate-to-account mappings, the authentication attributes, and the account records that associate credentials with identities. A misconfigured mapping can let an identity authenticate as someone else using a certificate it legitimately controls. A manipulated account attribute can redirect a valid credential to a different record. These are not proofing failures and not authentication failures. They are binding failures — and they are invisible to any review that checks the credential's validity without checking what the credential is bound to.

1.3.6 Identity, Authentication, and Federation Assurance Levels
Assurance is not a single rating. It is three separate measurements of three separate trust questions. Identity Assurance Level (IAL) measures the confidence established by the identity proofing process — whether the claimed identity corresponds to a specific real-world human. Authentication Assurance Level (AAL) measures the confidence of the authentication event — whether the entity presenting the authenticator is the legitimate subscriber. Federation Assurance Level (FAL) measures the protections applied to the assertion when identity information moves between an Identity Provider and a Relying Party.

The operative rule: IAL, AAL, and FAL are not interchangeable scores, and they do not combine into one number. A system can authenticate at a high AAL — a PIV-backed logon — while its identity was proofed years ago at a lower IAL, under weaker evidence rules, and never re-verified as the individual's role changed. The same environment can carry strong AAL and a weak FAL if its federation assertions are signed by a poorly protected key or validated without audience restrictions. Each level answers a different question, and an environment can be strong on one and weak on another simultaneously.

Each level also describes a different attack surface. IAL fails when authoritative data is stale or manipulated, because proofing conclusions rest on data the directory does not control. AAL fails when credentials, tickets, or sessions are stolen, because the authentication process cannot distinguish the legitimate subscriber from an attacker presenting the subscriber's material. FAL fails when signing keys are compromised, assertions are forged, or relying parties validate weakly. The defender's job is to know which level each mission function actually requires, and to test each level against the pathways that would defeat it.

Standards currency applies here throughout the book: NIST SP 800-63-4, finalized July 31, 2025, and its companion volumes 800-63A-4, 800-63B-4, and 800-63C-4 supersede the Revision 3 suite. The book treats the current suite as authoritative and retains Revision 3 references only where legacy systems or authorization records require them.

1.3.7 Authoritative Attributes, EDIPI, and Identity Data Integrity

An identity is more than an account name and a credential. It is a collection of attributes - affiliation, organization, employment status, sponsorship, role, eligibility - that other systems consume when making access decisions. Every attribute has an origin. An authoritative attribute source is the system approved as the trusted origin for a specific category of identity information: one system for personnel status, another for organizational assignment, another for application-specific roles. Active Directory stores and synchronizes many of these attributes, and it is frequently not the authority for any of them.

The failure mode this creates is one the directory cannot see. The directory can execute perfectly - the LDAP query succeeds, the attribute is returned, the group membership resolves, the authentication completes - while the identity decision derived from that data is factually incorrect, because the upstream source was stale, inaccurate, or manipulated. The problem isn't that the directory didn't execute correctly - it did. The problem is that the data it executed against was compromised. This is why attribute integrity is an identity security problem and not a data-quality problem: an attacker who can write to an attribute consumed by a federation service, a dynamic group, or an application role can change authorization across the enterprise without touching a single Access Control List (ACL).

The Electronic Data Interchange Personal Identifier (EDIPI) illustrates both the value and the limit of identity data. Commonly known as the DoD Identification Number, the EDIPI is a unique ten-digit personal identifier created for and assigned to a person entity with a direct relationship to the Department of Defense. It is intended to replace the Social Security Number (SSN) for many DoD business purposes, and an assigned EDIPI is not reused for another individual.

The Department of Defense stores the EDIPI in the Defense Enrollment Eligibility Reporting System (DEERS), a centralized identity and eligibility database maintained within the Defense Manpower Data Center (DMDC) environment. DEERS supports identity, personnel, eligibility and affiliation records for populations including active -duty service members, reservists, civilian employees, contractors, dependents, and other eligible beneficiaries. The Real-time Automated Personnel Identification System (RAPIDS) uses DEERS data during identity-card issuance. For eligible cardholders, RAPIDS supports issuance of a Common Access Card (CAC), on which the DoD identification number is represented through the cards printed and electronic identity data.

The EDIPI is not an authenticator. Possession or knowledge of the identifier neither authenticates nor authorizes; its security value depends entirely on correct binding to the correct identity record and accurate propagation across the systems that consume it. An EDIPI that is correctly bound and consistently synchronized is a governance asset. An EDIPI that drifts across records, is bound to the wrong person identity, or is treated as if knowing it proved identity becomes a liability.

Attribute governance therefore demands provenance, ownership, data-quality requirements, timely lifecycle updates, access and disclosure controls, reconciliation, and error correction. Every attribute consumed in an access decision should be traceable back to its origin or source, and every source should be under defined authoritative control - because the attribute is the point where a directory that behaves correctly can still produce an authorization that is fundamentally wrong.

1.3.8 Authentication, Federation, Authorization, and Access as a Trust Chain

The pathway from identity to resource access is not one decision alone. It is a chain of separate decisions made by separate systems under separate authority: identity recognition, authentication, assertion acceptance, attribute evaluation, authorization, session establishment, and resource access. Active Directory participates in several links - it authenticates principals, it supplies the attributes and group memberships that later links consume - but it does not own the chain. The Identity Provider, the federation service, the Relying Party, the application, and the resource each decide something independently.

Defenders routinely collapse these links into a single idea called "access." Adversaries never do. AN attacker can freely attack any single link: steal the credential to defeat and bypass authentication mechanisms, forge the assertion to defeat federation, manipulate the attribute to defeat evaluation, abuse the role mapping to defeat authorization, or hijack the session entirely to bypass everything upstream. Each attack is invisible to a review that examines only but one link. A strong authentication event with a forged token is still a successful session. A valid token with an overprivileged role mapping is still unauthorized access.

Two consequences follow. First, the chain is only as strong as its weakest link, and the weakest link is frequently the one no single team owns - the application's role mapping, the synchronization rule, the attribute source, the relying party's validation logic. Second, when the chain fails, attribution requires evidence at every link. The defender needs to know not only that access occurred, but which identity was recognized, which credential was presented, which assertion was accepted, which attributes were evaluated, and which authorization decision followed. Section 1.10 examines the evidence requirement; the point here is structural - the trust chain is the unit of analysis, and it crosses boundaries no single directory spans.

1.3.9 Why Identity Governance Must Be Tested Against Attack Pathways

Documented governance is intent. Implemented control is fact. The federal framework produces the first - policy, architecture, control selections, authorizations - and this book's central thesis is that the first is incomplete until the second has been validated against the pathways an adversary would normally use. A policy that states only administrators can reset passwords is not security. The implemented permissions, delegation, nested groups, and inherited rights determine the truth, and the truth is frequently different each time authentication occurs.

This is why governance must be tested against known or unknown attack pathways rather than against security checklists. The validation must reach actual permissions, credentials, delegation, synchronization, certificate, federation, and administrative control pathways - the relationships that determine who can really do what, not the roles that policy says should exist. Case Study 1-2 develops exactly this failure: an environment with current inventories, completed checklists, and documented controls that was nonetheless traversable through indirect identity control pathways no single compliance artifact revealed.

The discipline this book teaches is to connect the two. Every governance requirement should be paired with a validation question - how would this requirement be defeated, and what evidence would prove it holds? Every technical finding should be paired with a governance consequence - which policy, assurance level, authorization decision, or accountability obligation does it affect? That pairing is the method that is used throughout the remaining chapters, and it is the standard by which the reader should judge every assessment, every penetration test, and every continuous monitoring report they produce or receive.

1.4 Identity as Contested Terrain

Active Directory is contested terrain any which way you look at it. Adversaries are not only trying to simply enter a network; they are trying to exert influence over the identities, credentials, permissions, and trust relationships through which the enterprise devices who may act.

A conventional security assessment often begins with systems: workstations, servers, applications, network devices, and vulnerabilities. Those systems remain important, but an identity-centered assessment begins with authority. It asks which identities are trusted, how those identities authenticate, what permissions they receive, which systems accept their authority, and who or what can modify the mechanism that produce those decisions.

This perspective changes the meaning of compromise completely. AN attacker who compromises one workstation may gain an operating position. An attacker who compromises an identity may gain the ability to act as a recognized principal. An attacker who compromises an identity authority may gain the ability to alter what the enterprise recognizes as legitimate.

1.4.1 Why Adversaries Target Identity

Adversaries target identity because identity provides reach.

A compromised endpoint may expose files, processes, or local credentials. A compromised identity may provide access to multiple systems that already trust that identity. A compromised privileged identity may provide administrative authority across an entire domain, forest, cloud tenant, application environment, or mission-partner relationship.

Identity is attractive because it allows adversaries to operate through mechanisms the environment already accepts. The attacker does not always need to exploit a new software vulnerability or bypass every security control. If the attacker can obtain a valid credential, ticket, token, key, certificate, session, or delegated permission the hardest part of their job is already taken care of and the environment may process that activity as ordinary authenticated behavior.

The value of an identity depends on more than its job title or group name. An apparently ordinary service account may operate a synchronization engine. A computer account may have access to a certificate template or management service. A support identity may be able to reset the password of a more privileged account. A cloud group synchronized from Active Directory may control access to an application or administrative role.

Adversaries therefore target the relationships surrounding identity:

Which identities can authenticate?
Which identities can modify groups?
Which accounts can reset credentials?
Which systems can accept certificates or tokens?
Which service accounts operate across boundaries?
Which administrators share workstations or credentials?

The target is often not the identity itself. The target is the authority the identity can exercise.

1.4.2 Credentials, Tickets, Tokens, Certificates, Keys, and Sessions

Identity authority is represented by more than passwords.

A password may authenticate a user, but other artifacts may provide equivalent or greater operational value. These artifacts include Kerberos tickets, authentication tokens, certificates, private keys, smart cards, cached credentials, browser sessions, service-account secrets, federation assertions, cloud sessions, and machine-account credentials.

Each artifact represents a different form of trust.

A password may be changed, but an already-issued ticket may remain usable for some period. A user account may be disabled, but an active cloud session or application token may continue until it expires or is revoked. A certificate may remain cryptographically valid until expiration or revocation, even if the associated user account has changed. A private key may allow an adversary to produce authentication evidence without possessing the original password.

This creates an important defensive distinction: account state is not the same as credential state.

Disabling an account may prevent new authentication attempts while failing to invalidate every previously issued session, ticket, token, certificate, or delegated relationship. Similarly, changing a password may not address a compromised private key, federation signing key, service credential, or cloud application secret.

The defender must identify which authentication materials exist, where they are stored, how they are protected, how long they remain valid, and how they can be revoked or replaced during containment.

1.4.3 Access Rights Versus Control Pathways

Access and control are related; however, they are not the same.

An access right allows an identity to perform an action against a resource. A control pathway allows an identity or system to influence the identity, permission, credential, policy, or infrastructure that determines access.

The difference is significant.

An identity may not have access to a domain controller but may be able to modify a group whose members administer domain controllers. It may not be able to log on as a privileged user but may be able to reset that user's password. It may not be able to access a certificate authority directly but may be able to modify a certificate template or enrollment permission. It may not be a cloud administrator but may control a synchronized group that grants a cloud role.

These are control pathways.

A conventional inventory asks:

| What can this identity access?

An adversary asks:

| What can this identity change that would give it, or another identity it controls, additional access?

That question must also include indirect authority. Control may arise through group memberships, delegated permissions, object ownership, Group Policy, certificate enrollment, service administration, replication rights, virtualization, backup systems, synchronization platforms, or privileged workstations.

The distinction between access and control should guide both assessment and incident response efforts. Removing one access permission may not completely eliminate an adversary who still maintains control of the identity and who has power to recreate it. Disabling one account may not contain an attacker who has already established another credential or modified a downstream authorization source.

1.4.4 Trust Abuse Versus Conventional Exploitation

Conventional exploitation often involves taking advantage of a software flaw, insecure service, weak configuration, or exposed interface. Identity abuse may use none of those conditions in the obvious sense. It may involve the misuse of a legitimate trust relationship or authorized administrative function.

Examples include:

Using a valid account outside its approved purpose.
Abusing delegated rights to modify another identity.
Requesting a certificate through a permitted but dangerous enrollment path.
Using a legitimate service account from an unauthorized system.
Manipulating a trusted group whose membership control application access.
Altering an attribute consumed by a federation or cloud authorization rule.
Using a valid administrative session obtained from a compromised workstation.
Modifying a policy object through permissions that were granted for a legitimate operational reason.

The identity platform may process these actions correctly. It verifies the credential, evaluates the permission, issues the ticket or certificate, distributes the policy, or synchronizes the attribute. The failure is not necessarily in the mechanism. The failure is that an unauthorized actor has gained control of a mechanism the organization intended to reserve for an authorized purpose.

This is why identity security cannot be reduced to routine vulnerability scanning. A system may show no critical indicator or harbor a software vulnerability to show otherwise yet still contain a viable pathway from a low-privilege identity to a high-impact authority.

1.4.5 Why Identity Abuse Can Resemble Legitimate Authority

Identity abuse is difficult to detect because the adversary may use valid credentials, approved protocols, ordinary administrative tools, and legitimate management channels.

A domain controller may record a successful authentication. A file server may see a valid Kerberos ticket. An application may receive a correctly signed assertion. A certificate authroty may issue a certificate to a principal that technically possesses enrollment rights. A Group Policy change may appear to have been made by an account authorized to modify the policy.

The logs may accurately describe the transaction while failing to establish tat the legitimate owner intended it.

This creates a distinction between technical validity and operational legitimacy.

A valid ticket does not prove that the legitimate user requested it. A valid certificate does not prove that the authorized identity controlled the private key when it was used. A permitted directory modification does not prove that the change was approved. A successful federation transaction does not prove that the claims were accurate or that the relying application assigned an appropriate role.

Detection requires full context. Defenders must correlate identity, device, action, target, time, location, privilege, and expected behavior.

A privileged account authenticating from an approved administrative workstation during a scheduled maintenance window is different from the same account authenticating from a standard user workstation at an unusual time. A service account contacting its documented application server is different from that account initiating administrative activity against domain controllers. A certificate request that matches an approved enrollment workflow is vastly different from a sudden request for an authentication-capable certificate by an identity that has never performed that function.

Identity monitoring must ask not only whether an action was possible, but whether it was plausible, authrorized, and consistent with the identity's mission.

1.4.6 Why Endpoints Remain Important but Are Not the Entire Security Object

Endpoints remain essential to identity security because credentials, tickets, tokens, keys, certificates, and administrative sessions are frequently sued or exposed there. A compromised workstation may provide access to a user's session. A compromised server may expose service credentials. A compromised administrative workstation may provide a pathway to Tier 0.

Endpoint protection, therefore, remains a critical control.

But the endpoint is not the entire security object. Protecting a workstation does not correct excessive directory delegation. Protecting a domain controller does not secure a certificate authority whose certificate it trusts. Hardening an administrative workstation does not remove a synchronization pathway that can grant cloud authority. Monitoring endpoints does not reveal every dangerous group mapping, federation claim, or stale attribute.

Identity security is distributed across:

Directory services.
Domain controllers.
Administrative workstations.
Certificate authorities.
Federation services.
Cloud identity platforms.
Synchronization infrastructure.
Applications and relying parties.
Privileged access systems.
Virtualization and backup platforms.
Authoritative identity stores.
Mission-partner connections.

An endpoint may be where an adversary obtains the first credential, but the resulting authority may be defined elsewhere. Conversely, a weakness in a certificate template, group, federation rule, or synchronization service may create a pathway that reaches many endpoints without compromising them individually.

The correct question is not whether the endpoint is secure in isolation. It is whether the endpoint participates in a trustworthy identity control path.

1.4.7 Identity-Centric Mission Consequences

Identity abuse becomes mission-relevant when it affects the authority required to perform, protect, sustain, or recover mission-essential functions.

The consequence may be immediate. A compromised credential may allow unauthorized access to a mission application. A manipulated group may change who can view or modify operational data. A disabled federation service may prevent mission partners from accessing a shared capability. A failed certificate chain may interrupt smartcard authentication or encrypted communications.

The consequence may also be indirect. An adversary who compromises identity infrastructure may alter security policy, invalidate audit evidence, disrupt containment, compromise recovery accounts, or create uncertainty about which administrative actions were legitimate.

Identity compromise can therefore affect:

Mission-system availability.
Integrity of operational data.
Confidentiality of protected information.
Administrative control.
Partner and coalition access.
Accountability and attribution.
Incident containment.
Credential and certificate revocation.
Recovery and trust restoration.

Not every identity incident becomes a mission crisis. Impact depends on the identity function affected, the authority available through it, the systems that depend on it, the duration of the compromise, and the presence of independent controls.

The central analytical requirement is precision. Defenders must determine what failed, what authority the affected component possessed, which systems relied on it, what an adversary could do through the resulting pathway, what evidence remains trustworthy, and what mission functions could be disrupted or manipulated.

Identity is contested terrain because it is where organizational authority becomes technical authority. The enterprise expresses trust through accounts, credentials, certificates, tokens, attributes, groups, policies, and sessions. Adversaries contest that trust by stealing, manipulating, issuing, redirecting, or abusing those mechanisms.

The defender's mission is not simply to prevent unauthorized logon. It is to preserve the integrity of the decisions that determine who may act, what they may do, and whether the enterprise can continue to trust its own identity infrastructure after compromise.

1.5 Thinking Like an Attacker as a Defensive Discipline

Thinking like an attacker is not an invitation to abandon defensive responsibility. It is a disciplined method for discovering how legitimate identity functions can be misused before an adversary discovers the same pathways.

A defender who evaluates only whether a control exists may conclude that the environment is protected. An attacker asks a different question: what can be reached, changed, issued, delegated, impersonated, or controlled from the position already available?

That difference matters because identity environments rarely fail through one dramatic weakness. They fail through relationships. A low-privilege identity may expose directory information. That information may reveal a service account. The service account may control an application server. The server may contain an administrative session. The session may reach a privileged management system. The management system may influence the directory.

No single step necessarily appears catastrophic. The pathway becomes dangerous because each step enables the next.

1.5.1 Attacker Thinking Without Abandoning the Defender’s Mission

Attacker-oriented analysis begins with the defender’s responsibility: preserve the confidentiality, integrity, availability, accountability, and recoverability of mission systems. The attacker perspective is a means of testing that responsibility, not an alternative mission.

The defender does not ask how to cause harm for its own sake. The defender asks how an authorized adversary could:

Discover the identity environment.
Obtain or misuse an identity.
Expand authority.
Move through trusted relationships.
Establish persistence.
Affect mission systems.
Avoid or undermine detection.
Complicate containment and recovery.

This perspective exposes weaknesses that conventional compliance reviews often miss. A policy may require least privilege, but attacker-oriented analysis examines whether delegated permissions, nested groups, service identities, certificate enrollment, or management infrastructure defeat that objective in practice.

A control is not validated merely because it is documented. It is validated when the environment can demonstrate that the control prevents, detects, limits, or recovers from the pathway it was intended to address.

Thinking like an attacker also prevents defenders from relying on organizational assumptions. A person’s title does not determine technical authority. A system’s ownership does not determine its effective control. A defensive tool is not automatically low risk because its stated purpose is protection. The relevant question is always what the identity or system can actually do.














PART I – WHY FEDERAL ACTIVE DIRECTORY DOMAINS AND NETWORKS ARE DIFFERENT

CHAPTER 2. GOVERNING FICAM BODIES
CHAPTER ABSTRACT
This chapter introduces the federal organizations, laws, directives, mandates, frameworks, and governing authorities that shape federal cybersecurity, identity management, and Active Directory FICAM security throughout federal enterprises. Readers will learn how federal cybersecurity policy flows from legislative and executive mandates through standards organizations, operational commands, assessment authorities, and implementation teams. Understanding these governance structures is essential for cybersecurity professionals responsible for designing, assessing, defending, or operating Active Directory and FICAM infrastructure within the federal government.
Federal cybersecurity programs operate within a highly structured governance ecosystem consisting of legislative mandates, executive directives, regulatory authorities, standards organizations, operational commands, and assessment bodies. Unlike commercial enterprises, federal organizations must adhere to and align cybersecurity operations with statutory requirements, risk management processes, compliance frameworks, and Command mission assurance objectives established and set forth by numerous oversight entities and authoritative bodies.
Identity systems – including Active Directory, Active Directory Certificate Services (AD CS), Active Directory Federation Services (AD FS), Public Key Infrastructure (PKI), and Federal Identity, Credential, and Access Management (FICAM) programs – are directly influenced by these governance structures. Every decision involving authentication, authorization, privileged access, system and domain hardening, monitoring, logging, and incident response are frequently derived from federal policies, National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) guidance, DoD Directives, Presidential Executive Orders (EOs), DISA STIG security baselines and benchmarks, and operational requirements established by federal authorities.
This chapter examines the federal cybersecurity governance landscape and explains how federal agencies, organizations, and military branches such as Congress, Office of Management and Budget (OMB), National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA), the Department of Defense (DoD) Chief Information Officer (CIO), the Department of Navy (DoN) Chief Information Officer (CIO), Defense Information Systems Agency (DISA), National Security Agency (NSA), United States Cyber Command (USCYBERCOM), Joint-Force Headquarters-Department of Defense Information Network (JFHQ-DoDIN), FedRAMP, the Army, and the Air Force influence cybersecurity and identity management decisions. Readers will gain an understanding of how federal laws become policies, how policies become technical controls, and how technical controls ultimately affect Active Directory security engineering firsthand, and operational readiness across federal and military enterprises.
LEARNING OBJECTIVES
Upon completion of this chapter, readers will be able to:
•	Explain the federal cybersecurity governance hierarchy.
•	Identify major federal cybersecurity regulatory authorities.
•	Describe how federal laws influence identity security programs.
•	Understand the relationship between FISMA, RMF, and NIST guidance.
•	Explain how federal cybersecurity policies affect Active Directory security.
•	Describe the role of operational organizations and Commands responsible for defending federal network infrastructure.
•	Understand how governance requirements translate into technical security controls.
•	Recognize the connection between identity security and mission assurance.
