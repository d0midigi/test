PART I – WHY FEDERAL ACTIVE DIRECTORY DOMAINS AND NETWORKS ARE DIFFERENT
CHAPTER 1. THE CASE FOR FEDERAL ACTIVE DIRECTORY FICAM DOMAINS AND NETWORKS
CHAPTER PURPOSE
This chapter establishes the need for a specialized approach to securing, assessing, defending, and hunting threats within federal Active Directory environments. It introduces the concepts of Federal Identity, Credential, and Access Management (FICAM), explains why Active Directory remains a critical component of federal identity infrastructure, and demonstrates how Command mission assurance, compliance requirements, and advanced threat actors have transformed identity systems into high-value operational targets.
CHAPTER ABSTRACT
For more that two decades, Microsoft Active Directory has served as the identity foundation for government agencies, military branches and organizations, defense contractors, intelligence communities (ICs), and civilian federal enterprises. While numerous publications have examined Active Directory administration, security, and penetration testing, few have addressed the unique operational realities of federal federate identity infrastructures where Active Directory functions as only one component within a larger Federal Identity, Credential, and Access Management (FICAM) ecosystem.
Federal Active Directory environments differ significantly from commercial enterprise deployments. Identity systems within federal NIPRNet and SIPRNet networks must support Risk Management Framework (RMF) requirements, Federal Information Security Modernization Act (FISMA) mandates, NIST digital identity guidance, Public Key Infrastructure (PKI) trust architectures, Common Access Card (CAC) and Personal Identity Verification (PIV) authentication systems, continuous logging and monitoring programs, and mission assurance objectives. Consequently, attacks against Active Directory are no longer just limited to unauthorized access, privilege escalation, or lateral movement; they increasingly represent threats to operational readiness, mission execution, and national security objectives and initiatives.
This chapter introduces readers to the foundational concepts that are necessary for understanding federal identity systems and establishes the framework through which offensive security practitioners, defenders, assessors, and security engineers should evaluate Active Directory environments. Readers will examine the evolution of federal identity architecture, explore the relationship between Active Directory and FICAM, analyze common misconceptions regarding federal directory services, and understand why modern adversaries are increasingly targeting identity infrastructures rather than traditional network perimeters.
LEARNING OBJECTIVES
Upon completion of this chapter, readers will be able to:
•	Differentiate between commercial Active Directory deployments and federal Active Directory environments.
•	Explain the purpose and objectives of Federal Identity, Credential, and Access Management (FICAM).
•	Describe the role of Active Directory within federal identity ecosystems.
•	Understand how identity systems support mission assurance and operational readiness.
•	Recognize why modern adversaries target identity infrastructures.
•	Explain the relationship between Active Directory, PKI, federation services, and federal identity programs.
•	Identify key stakeholders responsible for federal identity security.
•	Understand how offensive and defensive security operations intersect with identity management objectives.

Asynchronous Transfer Mode (ATM)
The technology of Asynchronous Transfer Mode (ATM) was developed in the telephone and telecommunications (Telecom) industry in the 1980s at major telephone research laboratories. The goal was to develop a networking technology that could carry an integrated mix of traffic (voice, video, data). At one point, there were two competitors to be the technology of choice: Synchronous Transfer Mode (STM) and ATM. Asynchronous Transfer Mode was the eventual winner.

Multiprotocol Label Switching (MPLS)
The idea behind Multiprotocol Label Switching (MPLS) is simple. In datagram switching, each packet is treated as an independent element by routers. If one implements a Differentiated Services architecture, each independent packet is treated according to a policy specifically for its service class only. In MPLS, on the other hand, the basic unit is a “flow.” Packets belonging to a given source destination flow within an MPLS network that has labels appended to the packet that is used by routers along specified routing paths to relay the packet from router-to-router, hop-to-hop.

Advanced Encryption Standard (AES)
The Advanced Encryption Standard (AES) is the encryption standard approved in 2000 under the auspices of the US government, originally for civilian cryptographic use. In 2003, the United States government approved the use of AES for classified and secret information. Versions of AES for 192bit and 256bit keys are approved for Top Secret information. The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), which led the three-year approval process for AES, has certified in excess of 1000 products incorporating AES.

 YouSay Tomato, I Say Tomahto (Red and Green Apples)

Federal Active Directory (AD) environments face fundamentally different security and compliance requirements than commercial ones. While both sectors are targeted by attackers, federal assessments reveal distinct vulnerabilities that are shaped by rigid regulatory frameworks like FedRAMP and the NIST SP 800-53. 

Regulatory and Architectural Differences
88	Compliance and Agility: Federal FICAM networks heavily prioritize strict compliance with NIST and FBI CJIS Security Policy. Commercial Active Directory(AD) environments prioritize rapid feature deployment and operational flexibility, ease-of-use, often leading to technical debt.
89	Air-Gapping and Segmentation: Federal security assessments frequently feature highly segregated, segmented, isolated, or otherwise disconnected (air-gapped) environments to protect SIPRNet classified information systems. Commercial environments tend to embrace highly integrated hybrid-cloud and Software-as-a-Service (SaaS) infrastructures.
90	Legacy Constraints: Federal agencies are often burdened with legacy enterprise software that mandates the use of less secure, deprecated protocols, like NTLM and Unconstrained Delegation.

Common Vulnerabilities and Findings in Federal Assessments

Federal Active Directory Security Assessments (ADSA) routinely highlight several systemic flaws as defined by the CISA / NSA Top Ten Cybersecurity recommendations:

91	Excessive Domain Admin Rights
Audits regularly find over-provisioned "Domain Admin" accounts assigned to staff, contractors, and service accounts. Federal guidance heavily stresses strict adherence to the Principle of Least Privilege (PoLP) and Tiered Administration models to prevent lateral movement.

92	Legacy Protocol Abuse
Federal networks are often slower to deprecate legacy authentication protocols like NTLMv1 and LAN Manager (LM). Federal red team engagements frequently exploit this using Pass-the-Hash (PtH) or NTLM relay attacks to escalate domain privileges.

93	Active Directory Certificate Services (AD CS) Misconfigurations
Federal enterprises widely use AD CS for smart-card and hardware-based PIV/CAC authentication. Penetration testing frequently identifies vulnerable certificate templates (e.g., ESC1 or ESC8) that allow threat actors to forge administrative certificates.

94	Unconstrained Kerberos Delegation
Found in older agency applications, this misconfiguration permits a compromised service account to impersonate an administrator and request Kerberos Ticket Granting Tickets (TGT) to take over Domain Controllers.

95	Stale Accounts and Tier 0 Accounts
Audit findings frequently spotlight orphaned, inactive administrative accounts and poorly monitored Tier 0 assets, offering attackers an easy vector to maintain long-term, stealthy persistence.
