## The Early Days: Pre-Active Directory Era

I entered the cybersecurity, information security, and information technology field before Active Directory even existed - we're talking pre-1999. Back then, directory services meant **Novell NetWare**. That was it. We ran flat networks with Novell Directory Services (NDS), and honestly, it worked pretty damn well for what it was.

### The Great Migration: Novell to Active Directory

Then came the orders from headquarters: "Tear down your flat network and turn it into an Active Directory network."

**Our collective response:** "What the hell is an Active Directory?"

Nobody knew what we were doing. Active Directory was brand new, Microsoft had just released it, and we were being thrown into the deep end. The migration was an absolute bitch. I remember those freezing data center sessions that stretched for hours, sitting on conference calls with headquarters' IT department while they walked us through troubleshooting issues.

**The learning curve was brutal:**

- Promoting Windows Servers to Domain Controllers
- Understanding ADUC (Active Directory Users and Computers)
- Wrapping our heads around Organizational Units
- Learning Group Policy (Group Policy WHAT?)
- Figuring out domain trusts and replication

We were literally thrown to the wolves. You either figured it out fast or you lost your job. There was no middle ground, no gradual training program - just "here's the project, make it happen, good luck."

But once things settled down and we got past the initial chaos? **Active Directory became cool.** We started seeing what it could do - centralized authentication, Group Policy enforcement, single sign-on across the enterprise. It was a game-changer once we understood it.

**The Legendary Checkpoint Hot-Swap Story:**

Early in my time at Lockheed (3rd or 4th year post-college), my supervisor - a retired Master Chief - handed me a brand new hard drive and said: "Go swap out the bad drive in the firewall."

Our firewall was a Checkpoint R75 appliance running on Windows 2000 (yes, really) with dual HDDs in RAID. One drive had failed.

**What I did:**

- Walked to the server room
- Hot-swapped the drive while the firewall was running
- Waited for the RAID lights to go green
- Verified everything in the admin UI
- Walked back and handed him the dead drive
- Total time: ~5 minutes

**His reaction:**

- "You're done already?!"
- "Did you power down the firewall first?"
- Me: "Nooo... I didn't have to! They're hot-swappable."
- Him: _starts laughing_ "Next time, power it down properly before hardware maintenance."

**Why this was ballsy:**

- DoD production firewall
- Windows 2000 boot time: 35 minutes (nail-biting every single time)
- No change control ticket
- Just... did it
- Technically correct (RAID hot-swap exists for this reason)
- Organizationally questionable (procedure over efficiency in DoD environments)

The Master Chief didn't write me up. He laughed, taught me the proper procedure, and definitely told that story at every retirement party afterward.

## Key Career Transitions & Lessons Learned

### From Blue Team Compliance to Red/Purple Team Reality

I spent years in the world of FISMA, RMF, security control assessments, and policy documentation. I was the ISSO overseeing DoD Information Security, navigating the maze of policies, directives, and instructions. I lived and breathed Governance, Risk, and Compliance.

**The realization:** Checking boxes on security control spreadsheets doesn't stop actual attackers. Understanding NIST 800-53 is important, but it's not the same as understanding how an attacker dumps credentials from LSASS, crafts a Golden Ticket, or pivots through a network using Kerberos delegation abuse.

**The shift:** I went from pure defensive security and compliance work to becoming a red teamer specializing in offensive and defensive Active Directory security. I've executed authorized assessments performing:

- Kerberoasting attacks
- DCSync operations
- Pass-the-Hash/Pass-the-Ticket techniques
- Golden and Silver Ticket forgery
- Unconstrained delegation exploitation
- GPO abuse for lateral movement

Not to break things, but to understand _exactly_ how attackers operate so we can defend properly.

**Philosophy:** "To defend something, you must first know how to destroy it."

### From Solitary Work to Solo Hacker

After years in big DoD teams with bureaucracy and meetings, life happened. Three kids. Personal changes. A complete restructuring of priorities.

Now I work alone. Really alone. No meetings. No small talk. No performance reviews. Just me, my terminal, and the infinite rabbit holes of security research.

**What I learned about working in solitude:**

The brutal honesty - you can't hide from your own incompetence. In a team, you can delegate, coast, or nod along. Alone? If that Kerberos attack isn't working, you HAVE to figure out why. No one else will.

The deep work advantage - 12-hour solo deep-dives into Active Directory delegation vulnerabilities. No context switching. No Slack notifications. Just pure, uninterrupted research that produces the kind of depth most people never achieve.

The freedom to experiment - Build weird security tools with neon cyberpunk themes? Nobody's stopping me. Write 10,000 words on a single attack technique? Do it. The best research happens when you're completely alone with your thoughts and your demons.

## Personal Philosophy

**On Technical Depth:** If someone writes "code executed" without explaining what code, what technique, what actual steps - they're reciting buzzwords, not educating. I want protocol-level, code-level, system-level details. Anything less is security theater.

**On Learning:** I question everything. I don't accept hand-wavy "and then magic happens" explanations. Every technique, every vulnerability, every defense - I want to know the actual mechanics, the underlying protocols, the real reasons why things work or fail.

**On Perfectionism:** I have 2,000+ written articles, many still unpublished because of perfectionism and project completion challenges. I'm working on shipping good work instead of perfect work, because perfection is the enemy of progress.

**On Communication:** Direct, honest, no corporate bullshit. If I think something is wrong, I'll tell you. If I don't know something, I'll admit it and then go learn it. The security field has enough people pretending to know everything - we need more people willing to say "I don't know, but let's figure it out."
