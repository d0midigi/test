# 4.5.3 Federal Civilian Identity Security

CISA's role in federal identity security becomes particularly visible in Zero Trust implementation.

The agency's Zero Trust Maturity Model organizes modernization across five pillars—Identity, Devices, Networks, Applications and Workloads, and Data—with cross-cutting capabilities supporting visibility, analytics, automation, and governance. CISA's model is intended to help agencies plan and mature toward Zero Trust architecture rather than treat Zero Trust as a binary deployment state.

The structure reinforces a principle already established in this book: identity cannot be secured independently from the context in which it is exercised.

An account may be legitimate while the device is compromised.

A device may be compliant while the requested application access is excessive.

A user may authenticate strongly while the session context later changes.

A workload may be trusted for one function and inappropriate for another.

This does not diminish the importance of identity.

It reveals why identity is foundational without being sufficient by itself.

A Zero Trust architecture depends on reliable identity information because policy decisions are only as trustworthy as the identity, device, application, and contextual signals being evaluated.

If the directory says the wrong user belongs to a privileged group, the policy engine is beginning with bad state.

If a synchronization process supplies an incorrect attribute, downstream conditional access may enforce the wrong rule perfectly.

If an adversary controls a service principal, the cloud application may receive an entirely valid token for adversary-controlled authority.

The security policy can be functioning exactly as designed while enforcing corrupted identity information.

Once again, upstream authority matters.
