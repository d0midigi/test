# 4.1.1 Authority Becomes More Specific as It Approaches the Technology

At the highest level, federal cybersecurity requirements tend to describe obligations in terms of protection, accountability, risk, mission, and organizational responsibility.

As the requirement moves closer to implementation, the language becomes increasingly concrete.

FISMA requires agency-level information-security programs and the management of risks to agency information and systems. NIST's federal risk-management structure then provides a disciplined process for categorization, control selection, implementation, assessment, authorization, and continuous monitoring. FIPS 199 and FIPS 200 establish categorization and minimum security requirements within their applicable federal scope. The RMF then provides the structure through which those requirements are applied throughout the system life cycle.

By the time the requirement reaches Active Directory engineering, the implementation may look considerably more specific.

An access-control objective may become a restriction on who can modify privileged groups.

An identification-and-authentication requirement may affect smart-card authentication, account policy, Kerberos configuration, certificate mapping, or authenticator lifecycle.

An audit requirement may become Advanced Audit Policy, System Access Control List (SACL) configuration, directory-service-change auditing, centralized log forwarding, and defined detection logic.

A configuration-management requirement may become controlled Group Policy administration, baseline comparison, STIG validation, and configuration-drift monitoring.

The lower-level implementation does not replace the higher-level authority.

It operationalizes it.
