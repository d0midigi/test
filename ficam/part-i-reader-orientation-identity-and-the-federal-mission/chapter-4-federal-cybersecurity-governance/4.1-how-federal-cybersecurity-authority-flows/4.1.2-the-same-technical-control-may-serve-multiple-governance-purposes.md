# 4.1.2 The Same Technical Control May Serve Multiple Governance Purposes

Identity controls rarely map cleanly to one policy objective.

Consider a Privileged Access Workstation.

From an identity-security perspective, the workstation reduces the likelihood that privileged credentials are exposed to lower-trust systems.

From an access-control perspective, it constrains the context in which administrative authority can be exercised.

From an assume-breach perspective, it helps prevent compromise of an ordinary endpoint from becoming compromise of privileged identity.

From an RMF perspective, its implementation may contribute to several security-control objectives rather than one isolated control.

From a mission perspective, it protects the administrative plane on which critical identity services depend.

This is why federal cybersecurity engineering should not be approached as one technical setting per compliance requirement.

Good controls often satisfy several security objectives simultaneously because the underlying engineering decision reduces several forms of risk.

The reverse is also true.

A single technical weakness can undermine several governance objectives at once.

An overprivileged identity-management account may weaken least privilege, separation of duties, credential protection, account management, authorization governance, monitoring expectations, and mission assurance even though the weakness appears technically as one directory principal with excessive rights.

The governance framework divides the problem into manageable requirements.

The attack path puts those requirements back together.
