# 4.4.1 FIPS

Federal Information Processing Standards sit closer to the mandatory standards layer than ordinary technical guidance.

For the security architecture developed throughout this book, three FIPS publications are particularly important: FIPS 199, FIPS 200, and FIPS 201.

FIPS 199 establishes a common method for security categorization based on the potential impact to confidentiality, integrity, and availability if federal information or information systems are compromised. That categorization matters to identity infrastructure because the consequences of identity failure are inherited by systems that depend upon it. A directory service supporting many high-impact mission functions cannot be evaluated as though its only consequence were temporary loss of an administrative server.

FIPS 200 establishes minimum security requirements across defined security-related areas and connects those requirements to the use of appropriate security controls. The standard is intentionally broader than any one technology. It does not prescribe the exact access control entry that should exist on an Organizational Unit or the precise Kerberos policy that should be configured on a Domain Controller. Instead, it establishes security requirements that must be satisfied through system-specific implementation.

That distinction is important for Active Directory security.

A requirement for access control may eventually become group design, delegated permissions, role separation, authentication restrictions, certificate issuance policy, privileged workstation requirements, or enforcement within an application. A requirement for audit and accountability may become directory-service auditing, Advanced Audit Policy, System Access Control Lists, centralized collection, and detection engineering.

The FIPS requirement remains stable while the technical implementation depends on the architecture.

FIPS 201 introduces a different but closely related federal identity problem: Personal Identity Verification. PIV establishes a government-wide credentialing standard for federal employees and contractors and therefore connects identity proofing, credential issuance, PKI, physical and logical access, and authentication assurance to technical systems such as Active Directory.

For the purposes of _Contested Terrain_, FIPS 201 matters because a federal identity environment cannot be understood solely from the directory outward.

Part of the identity trust chain begins before the domain account authenticates.

The credential itself has provenance.
