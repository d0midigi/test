# 4.2.2 National Security System Authorities

Federal cybersecurity governance changes when the system in question is a National Security System.

NIST develops federal cybersecurity standards and guidelines primarily under authorities applicable to non-NSS federal systems. For National Security Systems, policy authority is exercised through the national-security governance structure, including the Committee on National Security Systems. At the same time, the federal government has deliberately developed common risk-management foundations so the civilian, defense, and intelligence communities do not operate entirely incompatible cybersecurity models. NIST describes CNSS as providing policy, directives, and instructions for NSS while authorizing use of common NIST-developed risk-management guidance and adding requirements needed for national-security environments.

That relationship matters greatly in Department of Defense environments.

DoD practitioners may encounter NIST SP 800-37, NIST SP 800-53, CNSS Instruction 1253, DoD RMF policy, DISA implementation guidance, and system-specific requirements within the same authorization environment. DISA's own technical guidance describes DoD RMF policy as implementing the common NIST and CNSS risk-management foundation across DoD technology, including NSS and non-NSS contexts as applicable under DoD policy.

The practical lesson is not that NSS governance is simply “stricter NIST.”

That description would erase the policy distinction.

The better model is that federal communities share substantial risk-management architecture while different authorities determine applicability and additional requirements for different classes of systems.

For identity security, this distinction becomes operationally significant when the same practitioner supports multiple enclaves.

A NIPRNet identity service and a SIPRNet identity service may use similar Windows architecture.

The governance, mission impact, classification consequences, connection requirements, data-handling rules, and authorization constraints surrounding them are not interchangeable.

Technical familiarity with Active Directory does not eliminate those differences.
