# 4.2.1 FISMA

The Federal Information Security Modernization Act of 2014 is a central statutory foundation for civilian federal information-security governance and updated the earlier Federal Information Security Management Act framework created in 2002. NIST describes FISMA as emphasizing risk management rather than a checklist model, while the statute assigns agencies responsibility for developing, documenting, and implementing agency-wide information-security programs and for managing risk to the information and information systems supporting their operations and assets.

That distinction—risk management rather than checklist compliance—is particularly important for identity security.

Active Directory environments can be technically compliant with numerous individual requirements while retaining a dangerous attack path.

Suppose privileged accounts are strongly authenticated, Domain Controllers are hardened, security logging is enabled, and administrative groups are reviewed individually. Those are all valuable controls.

Yet a low-privileged delegated account may retain `WriteDACL` over an object that ultimately controls a privileged group.

None of the surrounding controls makes that path safe.

The relevant risk emerges from the composition of authority.

FISMA's risk-management orientation therefore aligns naturally with identity attack-path analysis. The objective is not simply to accumulate evidence that individual security activities occurred. The objective is to manage the actual risk created by the operation of the system.

For the Active Directory practitioner, this means that evidence must ultimately survive technical scrutiny.

An authorization package cannot make an unsafe permission safe.

A closed finding cannot make a control path disappear.

A compliant scanner result cannot prove that effective administrative authority is constrained correctly.

Governance only becomes meaningful when the represented security state matches the operational one.

#### FISMA and the Federal Standards Structure

FISMA's statutory framework is one reason NIST standards and risk-management guidance occupy such a significant place in federal cybersecurity.

FIPS 199 provides the standard for security categorization of federal information and information systems according to potential impact on confidentiality, integrity, and availability. FIPS 200 establishes minimum security requirements and connects those requirements to risk-based control selection. NIST's RMF provides the broader process through which controls are selected, implemented, assessed, authorized, and continuously monitored.

The practical result for identity infrastructure is that Active Directory does not receive a meaningful security posture merely because it is “an enterprise service.”

Its protection has to reflect the systems and missions that depend upon it.

Where Active Directory supplies authentication, authorization, administrative control, or common identity services to many systems, the consequences of its compromise extend through those dependencies.

This becomes especially important when identity services operate as common controls or shared services.

The security posture of one identity platform may influence the risk posture of many inheriting systems.

A single weakness in the common identity plane can therefore become an agency- or command-level problem rather than an isolated system finding.
