# 2.2.1 Windows NT Domains

Before Active Directory, Microsoft Windows enterprise identity was built largely around Windows NT domains.

The NT domain model provided centralized authentication and account management through Domain Controllers, but its structure was comparatively flat. Accounts and groups existed within domains without the hierarchical directory organization later introduced through Active Directory Organizational Units.

The model also relied heavily on Security Accounts Manager-style account databases and trust relationships between domains when organizations needed users in one domain to access resources in another.

As environments grew, those relationships became difficult to manage.

Large enterprises sometimes deployed multiple account domains, resource domains, and trust arrangements intended to separate administration or accommodate geography and organizational structure. The resulting trust relationships could become complex long before modern identity attack-path terminology existed.

This is important because one of the recurring lessons of enterprise identity is that complexity often appears first as an administrative solution and only later reveals itself as a security problem.

Windows NT solved legitimate operational requirements, but large deployments exposed the limitations of maintaining identity and trust at increasing scale.

Active Directory emerged in part to address those limitations.
