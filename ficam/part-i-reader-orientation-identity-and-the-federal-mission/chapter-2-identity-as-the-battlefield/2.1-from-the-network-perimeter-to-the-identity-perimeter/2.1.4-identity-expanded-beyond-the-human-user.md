# 2.1.4 Identity Expanded Beyond the Human User

The transition toward identity-centric security also expanded the definition of what counts as an identity.

Traditional discussions often begin with user accounts because human authentication is familiar. Modern enterprise environments contain far more identities than human users.

Computers possess identities.

Services possess identities.

Applications possess identities.

Automation possesses identities.

Cloud workloads possess identities.

Certificates can represent identities.

Managed service accounts operate as identities.

Service principals operate as identities.

Devices may possess cryptographically bound identities.

Federated partners assert identities.

Mission-partner environments may introduce identities governed outside the local agency or command.

These non-person identities can possess significant authority and often receive less lifecycle attention than human accounts.

A service identity may exist for years without meaningful review because changing it risks an application outage. An automation account may gradually accumulate permissions as additional tasks are assigned. A synchronization identity may receive privileges across multiple identity planes. A service principal may be trusted by applications without the visibility normally applied to an interactive administrator.

As a result, the identity perimeter is not simply the point where a person logs in.

It is the collection of trust decisions governing every principal capable of influencing enterprise resources.
