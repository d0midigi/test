# 2.1.2 Authentication Became Portable

One of the most significant changes was that authentication ceased to be tightly coupled to a single physical or network location.

A credential could be presented from many locations.

A Kerberos ticket could represent authority independently of the user's original password entry. A certificate could authenticate its holder without the password associated with the represented account. A federation token could convey an identity assertion from one system to another. A cloud refresh token could preserve access without repeatedly presenting the original authenticator. A service account could authenticate from numerous servers. An application credential could operate without a human user at all.

Trust became portable because the artifact representing the identity became portable.

This has enormous security implications.

When an attacker steals a valid authentication artifact, the attacker may not need to defeat the network boundary in the traditional sense. They may instead use an existing pathway the enterprise intentionally provides to authenticated users.

The result can look legitimate at the network layer.

The adversary is not necessarily forcing an unauthorized connection through the firewall. They may be presenting an authentication artifact to a service that is designed to accept it.

The battle therefore shifts from simple reachability toward authenticity, provenance, authorization, and context.
