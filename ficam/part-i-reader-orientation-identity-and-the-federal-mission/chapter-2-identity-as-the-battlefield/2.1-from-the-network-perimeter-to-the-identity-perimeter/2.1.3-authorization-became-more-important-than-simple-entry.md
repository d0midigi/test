# 2.1.3 Authorization Became More Important Than Simple Entry

As identity became increasingly central to access, authorization assumed greater strategic importance.

The most consequential question was no longer merely whether a principal could authenticate.

It was what happened next.

A compromised domain user who can authenticate but possesses little useful authority may provide an adversary with reconnaissance opportunities but limited immediate impact.

A compromised administrative account may permit direct system control.

A compromised service identity may expose applications or data unavailable to ordinary users. A principal with seemingly modest permissions over a sensitive directory object may possess an indirect path to much greater authority.

A certificate enrollment permission may enable an attacker to obtain authentication as a different principal.

A replication right may effectively expose domain credentials without requiring membership in Domain Admins.

The security significance of identity therefore resides in the relationship between authentication and authorization.

Authentication establishes a principal.

Authorization determines the consequences of trusting that principal.

The remainder of this book repeatedly returns to that distinction because many severe Active Directory compromises begin with completely legitimate authentication followed by authorization relationships whose consequences were poorly understood.
