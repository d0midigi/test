# 2.5.5 Identity Trust Is Only as Strong as Its Upstream Authorities

Every identity decision has dependencies.

A user account may depend on an authoritative personnel source, provisioning process, directory object, credential, authentication service, group membership, and resource authorization.

A certificate-backed identity adds Certification Authorities, enrollment policy, mapping, revocation, and key protection.

A federated identity adds issuer configuration, signing keys, claims transformation, and relying-party trust.

A hybrid identity may add synchronization engines, service accounts, cloud roles, connectors, and application registrations.

The final application sees the result.

The attacker studies the chain.

That difference in perspective matters.

Defenders often focus on the enforcement point because it is where access is granted. Adversaries frequently look upstream for a weaker authority capable of influencing that enforcement point indirectly.

A resource may be exceptionally well hardened while still trusting an identity produced through a vulnerable upstream process.

An administrator may have strong multifactor authentication while belonging to a group whose membership can be modified by a weaker delegated principal.

A certificate-authenticated account may use phishing-resistant credentials while a certificate template allows an unintended principal to obtain equivalent authentication authority.

The strength of the final authentication ceremony cannot compensate for uncontrolled authority upstream.

This is why the remainder of Contested Terrain treats identity as a chain of trust rather than a logon event.

Once trust is viewed as a chain, the offensive problem becomes clearer.

An adversary does not have to break every link.

Only one link must provide enough leverage to reach the next.
