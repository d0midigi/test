# 2.4.1 Vulnerabilities and Identity Weaknesses Are Not the Same Thing

Traditional vulnerability management is frequently organized around identifiable defects: missing patches, known Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures (CVEs), insecure versions, exposed services, weak protocols, or configuration findings.

Identity compromise certainly includes those problems, but some of the most consequential identity weaknesses do not map cleanly to a software flaw.

Suppose a user has permission to modify the membership of a privileged group.

Nothing is malfunctioning.

The directory is correctly enforcing an authorization decision.

The weakness lies in the assignment of authority.

The same is true when a service account has a weak static password, when an old trust remains active after a migration, when a certificate template permits an unsafe enrollment combination, or when a synchronization account possesses more privilege than its function requires.

The systems may be operating exactly as configured.

The configuration itself creates the attack path.

This difference is one reason identity security cannot be reduced to vulnerability scanning.

A scanner is well suited to finding missing patches or known configuration deviations. It may be much less effective at determining that an apparently ordinary user controls a group that controls another group that controls a GPO that ultimately affects Domain Controllers.

The dangerous property emerges from relationships among otherwise legitimate objects.
