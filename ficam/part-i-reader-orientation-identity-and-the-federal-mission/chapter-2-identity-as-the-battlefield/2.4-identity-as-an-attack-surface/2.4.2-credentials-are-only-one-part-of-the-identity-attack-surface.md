# 2.4.2 Credentials Are Only One Part of the Identity Attack Surface

Credential theft receives substantial attention because credentials provide an obvious route into identity systems.

Passwords, NT hashes, Kerberos keys, certificates, private keys, cached credentials, tokens, session cookies, and other authenticators can all become valuable to an adversary.

But credential possession is only one way to obtain identity authority.

An attacker may instead manipulate authorization.

If a compromised principal can reset another user's password, add itself to a group, change an object's owner, modify a DACL, or alter a certificate template, the adversary may obtain greater authority without stealing an additional credential first.

This matters because defensive programs often concentrate heavily on protecting secrets while underestimating the permissions capable of manufacturing new authority.

Credential protection asks whether the adversary can steal an existing means of authentication.

Authorization security must also ask whether the adversary can create or assign one.

That distinction becomes particularly important in Active Directory Certificate Services, delegated administration, Group Policy, key trust, and hybrid identity.

The attacker does not always need to find the key.

Sometimes the attacker can change the lock.
