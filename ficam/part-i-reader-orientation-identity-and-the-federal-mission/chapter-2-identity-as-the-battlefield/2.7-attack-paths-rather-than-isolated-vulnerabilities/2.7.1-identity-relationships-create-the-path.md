# 2.7.1 Identity Relationships Create the Path

Active Directory is particularly suited to attack-path analysis because so much of its security model is relational.

Users belong to groups.

Groups may belong to other groups.

Principals possess permissions over objects.

Objects inherit permissions from containers.

Computers contain sessions.

Services operate under accounts.

Accounts possess Service Principal Names.

Group Policy Objects apply to systems.

Trust relationships connect domains and forests.

Certificates map to identities.

Management platforms control endpoints.

Synchronization systems connect identity planes.

Each relationship can be represented as an edge between entities.

Most edges are legitimate and necessary.

The security problem emerges when a sequence of legitimate edges produces unintended effective authority.

This is why graph-based analysis became so valuable in Active Directory security. A graph can expose relationships that are difficult to perceive through conventional administrative interfaces.

An administrator reviewing a group may see its direct members.

An attack graph may show that a low-privileged user controls another group that can modify that group.

An administrator reviewing a computer may see its local administrators.

An attack graph may show that one of those administrators has an active session elsewhere and that another principal can reach the first system.

The graph does not invent new permissions.

It reveals how existing permissions compose.
