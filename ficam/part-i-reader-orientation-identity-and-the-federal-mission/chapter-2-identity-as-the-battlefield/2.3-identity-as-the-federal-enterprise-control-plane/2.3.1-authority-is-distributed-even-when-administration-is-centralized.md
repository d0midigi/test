# 2.3.1 Authority Is Distributed Even When Administration Is Centralized

Active Directory is frequently described as centralized identity management. Operationally, that description is useful. Administrators can manage users, groups, computers, policy, and authentication through a common directory architecture instead of configuring every system independently.

Technically, however, the resulting authority is not concentrated in one place.

It is distributed.

A group created in the directory may be consumed by a file server, database, application, network appliance, cloud integration, virtualization platform, or custom business system. The directory maintains the identity relationship, but the receiving system interprets that relationship within its own authorization model.

The same distinction applies to authentication. A Domain Controller may validate an account and participate in Kerberos ticket issuance, but the service receiving that ticket still makes a local decision about whether the authenticated principal is permitted to access the requested resource.

This distribution explains why identity compromise spreads differently from compromise of an ordinary endpoint.

A workstation compromise begins with authority over a workstation.

A trusted identity compromise can alter the information that many systems use to make their own access decisions.

The distinction is subtle but strategically important.

If an attacker takes control of a file server, the attacker controls the data and capabilities available through that server.

If the attacker obtains control over a group that numerous file servers use for authorization, the attacker may influence access across all of them without compromising each server independently.

If the attacker gains authority over the identity system responsible for creating or modifying those groups, the problem becomes broader still.

The compromise has moved upstream.

This upstream position is why identity-control-plane assets deserve different treatment from ordinary enterprise systems.

Their value lies not only in what they contain, but in what other systems are willing to believe about the information they produce.
