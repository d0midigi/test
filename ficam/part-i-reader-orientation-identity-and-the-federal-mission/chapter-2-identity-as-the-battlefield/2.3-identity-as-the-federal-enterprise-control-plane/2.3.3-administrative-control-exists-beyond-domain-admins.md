# 2.3.3 Administrative Control Exists Beyond Domain Admins

Domain Admin membership is highly visible, which makes it a convenient shorthand for privilege. It is not a complete model of Active Directory authority.

An environment can contain principals with extraordinary control that are not members of Domain Admins, Enterprise Admins, or another obviously privileged group.

Delegated administration creates many such cases deliberately.

A help-desk group may be allowed to reset passwords within a particular Organizational Unit. An application team may be allowed to manage service-account objects. A server-administration group may control computer objects or Group Policy within a defined scope. A certificate team may administer templates or Certification Authority configuration. An identity-management platform may possess permissions required to provision accounts across large portions of the directory.

These are legitimate operational arrangements.

Their security significance depends on what those permissions can ultimately influence.

A principal does not need Domain Admin membership if it can modify an object that leads to equivalent authority.

A delegated group capable of resetting the password of an administrator possesses authority over that administrator's identity.

A principal capable of modifying membership in a group that administers Domain Controllers may possess a path to the domain even though its own account appears unprivileged.

An account capable of changing the Discretionary Access Control List (DACL) on a sensitive object may be able to grant itself additional rights.

An identity-management system capable of provisioning privileged memberships may possess administrative authority that is operationally equivalent to the identities it manages.

The concept that matters is therefore not privileged-group membership alone.

It is effective control.

Effective control captures the authority a principal can exercise after considering direct permissions, delegated rights, group relationships, inherited access, control over credentials, and indirect paths through other objects.

This is the model later attack-path chapters will formalize.
