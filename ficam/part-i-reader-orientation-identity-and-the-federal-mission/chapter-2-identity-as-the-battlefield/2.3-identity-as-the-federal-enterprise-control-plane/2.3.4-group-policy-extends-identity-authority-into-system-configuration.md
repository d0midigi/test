# 2.3.4 Group Policy Extends Identity Authority Into System Configuration

Group Policy illustrates how deeply the identity control plane can extend into operating-system behavior.

A Group Policy Object (GPO) is stored and administered through Active Directory and SYSVOL, but its effects occur on the systems processing the policy. Depending on configuration, Group Policy can influence security settings, registry values, scripts, services, firewall rules, scheduled tasks, user rights, auditing, software behavior, and numerous other operating-system controls.

This makes GPO administration considerably more powerful than its familiar administrative interface may suggest.

A principal that can modify the right GPO may gain the ability to change the security state of every system to which that policy applies.

The implication becomes especially serious when the policy applies to privileged workstations, servers, administrative infrastructure, or Domain Controllers.

Again, the attacker does not necessarily need a software exploit.

If authorization allows the modification, the directory and policy infrastructure will perform the distribution legitimately.

The control plane becomes the delivery mechanism.

This is a recurring characteristic of identity attacks: the adversary may exploit the authority of the system rather than defeat the system.
