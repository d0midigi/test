# 2.8.4 Authority Is the More Durable Adversary Objective

Credentials are valuable because they provide access to authority.

They are usually not the strategic objective by themselves.

An attacker does not need a particular administrator's password if another path provides equivalent control. If a certificate can represent the administrator, the password becomes less important. If a delegated permission can reset the administrator's password, stealing the existing password may be unnecessary. If control of a Group Policy Object can execute code on the systems the administrator manages, direct account takeover may not be required at all.

This changes how defenders should think about credential-focused security.

Protecting credentials remains critical. But a mature identity-security program protects the mechanisms through which authority is assigned, inherited, delegated, represented, and extended.

That includes groups, access-control lists, certificate issuance, key trust, federation, synchronization, management platforms, service identities, administrative workstations, privileged-access systems, and the directory itself.

The adversary's durable objective is not possession of a specific secret.

It is control over trusted authority.

Once that distinction is understood, many later techniques in _Contested Terrain_ stop appearing as separate categories of attack. Credential theft, ACL abuse, delegation abuse, certificate escalation, replication abuse, federation compromise, and hybrid identity escalation are different methods of reaching the same strategic condition: the ability to make trusted systems accept adversary-controlled authority.
