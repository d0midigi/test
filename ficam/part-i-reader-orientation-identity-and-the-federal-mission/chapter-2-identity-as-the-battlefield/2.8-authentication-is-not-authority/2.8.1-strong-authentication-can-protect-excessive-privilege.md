# 2.8.1 Strong Authentication Can Protect Excessive Privilege

Consider an administrative account protected by phishing-resistant multifactor authentication.

From an authentication perspective, this is a substantial improvement over a reusable password.

From an authorization perspective, nothing has necessarily changed.

If the account remains a member of unnecessarily broad privileged groups, can log on to lower-trust systems, administers unrelated infrastructure, or possesses control paths across multiple security planes, the underlying authority remains excessive.

The stronger authenticator reduces the probability that an attacker can impersonate the account through certain credential attacks.

It does not reduce what happens if the account is compromised through another means.

This distinction becomes especially important in federal environments where assurance levels appropriately receive substantial attention. Authenticator assurance addresses confidence in the authentication mechanism. It is not a statement that every permission granted after authentication is appropriate.

An AAL3-authenticated identity can still be overprivileged.

A valid CAC-authenticated session can still be misused.

A certificate can provide strong cryptographic proof of identity while the represented principal possesses dangerous authorization.

Authentication strength and authorization correctness solve different problems.
