# 2.6.1 Compromise Should Not Automatically Become Trust

Traditional enterprise architectures often accumulated implicit trust because it made administration easier.

A domain-joined computer was treated as safer because it belonged to the domain. An internal connection was treated differently from an external connection. An authenticated account gained broad visibility because authentication itself was considered sufficient proof of legitimacy. An administrator logging on with valid credentials was permitted to reach numerous systems because the account was privileged.

Each assumption made operational sense in the context in which it was created.

The problem appears after compromise.

Malware operating on a domain-joined workstation inherits the workstation's network position. An adversary using a stolen password inherits the account's authentication capability. A stolen administrative session inherits whatever access the administrator would ordinarily possess. A compromised service account may continue authenticating exactly as the application did before the compromise.

The infrastructure does not inherently know that control of the identity has changed hands.

This is why identity attacks can be so difficult to distinguish from legitimate activity. The attacker frequently enters the trust model rather than bypassing it.

An assume-breach architecture seeks to reduce the amount of additional trust granted merely because one prior security decision succeeded. Successful domain authentication should not imply unrestricted administrative reach. Possession of a valid user credential should not make privileged authentication material available. Compromise of a workstation should not expose credentials capable of controlling Domain Controllers. Membership in one administrative role should not automatically create authority over unrelated control planes.

The objective is to prevent one valid identity decision from becoming a blanket authorization for subsequent trust.
