# 1.8 The Book’s Progression

The structure of Contested Terrain follows the same progression the reader will eventually use when analyzing an unfamiliar identity environment.

Part I establishes the reader's perspective and the federal mission context. It explains why identity deserves treatment as contested infrastructure, where FICAM and DoD ICAM fit, and how federal governance converts identity requirements into technical obligations.

Part II moves into the architecture itself. The reader learns the directory, networking, authentication, authorization, credential, privilege, policy, trust, certificate, federation, and hybrid mechanisms that collectively establish identity authority. This is the technical terrain on which every later operation depends.

Part III changes from understanding the environment to assessing it. Architecture becomes inventory. Permissions become relationships. Trust becomes attack surface. The reader learns how to scope an identity assessment, establish a baseline, conduct passive reconnaissance, perform active enumeration, and construct attack paths without immediately confusing discovery with exploitation.

Part IV follows adversarial progression through the identity system. Initial access develops into credential acquisition, authority expansion, lateral mobility, trust traversal, persistence, and domain dominance. The focus remains on the mechanisms that make each transition possible rather than on artificially forcing every intrusion into a rigid linear sequence.

Part V turns the same terrain around. The directory structures and trust mechanisms exploited in Part IV are revisited from the defender's position. Administrative isolation, privileged access, account lifecycle, authentication hardening, policy security, certificate governance, federation security, hybrid identity, and attack-path reduction become engineering problems rather than attack opportunities.

Part VI addresses the point where preventive architecture is no longer enough. Telemetry, detection engineering, threat hunting, forensics, incident response, containment, resilience, and trust restoration are examined as interconnected capabilities. A mature identity program must be able not only to make compromise difficult, but to recognize when trust has failed and reconstruct that trust safely.

Part VII closes with operational lessons and emerging identity problems. The objective is not speculative futurism. It is to identify how machine identity, adaptive authentication, artificial intelligence, passwordless systems, cryptographic change, and Zero Trust modernization alter the same fundamental questions of identity, authority, and trust developed throughout the book.

The progression is therefore not simply offense followed by defense.

It is understanding followed by assessment, assessment followed by adversarial validation, adversarial validation followed by engineering, and engineering followed by the ability to detect and recover when prevention fails.
