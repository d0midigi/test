# 1.7.4 Authorization and Rules of Engagement

Authorization is the boundary separating legitimate offensive security operations from unauthorized intrusion. Technical capability, professional credentials, employment within an agency or command, possession of administrative access, or responsibility for defending a system does not independently establish authority to perform offensive testing against that system or network for which the reader does not explicitly own. Authorization must originate from an entity empowered to permit the activity and must define the systems, identities, techniques, conditions, and operational boundaries under which the assessment, or hacking will occur.

This distinction becomes especially important in Active Directory and FICAM environments because the apparent target of a security test may not correspond to the actual reach of the technique that is being exercised. A test that is directed to one account can trigger enterprise account lockout controls and thresholds. A Group Policy Object modification can quickly propagate to systems outside the immediate testing objective. A certificate issued by an enterprise Certificate Authority (CA) may authenticate against numerous systems that were never intended to participate in the assessment. A forest trust may expose another administrative boundary. An Active Directory Federation Services (AD FS) configuration may affect relying parties maintained by another team or agency. Microsoft Entra ID synchronization, application consent, cross-tenant access, and federation can all extend the consequences of an action beyond the on-premises environment entirely.

Clear instruction, guidance, and rules of engagement are very much part of the game, and are very necessary in preventing security testers from facing legal consequence.

For this reason, scope cannot be treated simply as just a list of Internet Protocol (IP) addresses, or hostnames, or even an asset inventory.

In identity-centric assessments, scope must account for trust. Period.

A Domain Controller may be in scope while a connected Certification Authority is not. A domain may be authorized for assessment while a trusted forest belongs to another command. An Entra Connect server may reside inside the approved network boundary while the Microsoft Entra tenant with which it synchronizes falls under separate administrative authority. A mission-partner identity may appear in the local directory while the originating identity provider belongs to another agency, coalition partner, contractor, or external service.

The operator therefore has to distinguish what can technically be reached from what has actually been authorized for testing.

Rules of Engagement (RoE) turn that authorization into operational direction. They establish how the assessment will be conducted rather than simply stating that an assessment may occur. Effective RoE should make clear what is permitted, what is prohibited, what requires additional coordination, what conditions require testing to stop, and how unexpected effects are handled.

For identity-security operations, those distinctions can become highly specific.

A penetration test may authorize Active Directory enumeration but prohibit directory-object modification. Password spraying may be permitted only below a defined threshold designed to avoid account lockout. Kerberoasting may be authorized because requesting service tickets is non-destructive, while attempting to crack a recovered credential belonging to a particular mission system may require separate approval. An assessment may permit certificate-template enumeration but prohibit requesting an authentication certificate from a production Certification Authority. DCSync permissions may be evaluated through access-control analysis without allowing the assessor to replicate production credential material. A red team may be authorized to demonstrate that a Group Policy Object could produce remote execution while being prohibited from modifying the actual production GPO.

These distinctions do not make an assessment less realistic. They allow the technical objective to be demonstrated while controlling consequences that provide little additional security value.

The RoE should also address operational deconfliction. Federal and military networks may be monitored simultaneously by local defenders, Cybersecurity Service Providers (CSSPs), enterprise Security Operations Centers (SOCs), network operations teams, counterintelligence elements, incident-response personnel, or other defensive organizations. A realistic red team exercise may deliberately withhold certain details from defenders, while a cooperative penetration test may require the SOC to know precisely when testing begins. Both arrangements can be legitimate. What matters is that the decision is deliberate and documented rather than discovered during the engagement.

Identity assessments create additional deconfliction concerns because offensive behavior frequently resembles genuine account compromise. Password spraying, unusual Kerberos ticket requests, LDAP enumeration, privilege-group modifications, certificate enrollment, remote administrative sessions, or replication activity can generate alerts that appropriately trigger defensive action. If the engagement design does not establish how those alerts should be handled, a security test can unintentionally consume incident-response resources, contaminate investigative evidence, or initiate containment procedures against production identities.

The Rules of Engagement should therefore establish escalation and stop conditions before testing begins.

If a test unexpectedly affects authentication availability, produces widespread account lockout, crosses into an unauthorized domain, reveals access to classified or otherwise restricted information outside the assessment requirement, impacts a mission-essential application, reaches an external mission partner, or uncovers evidence suggesting an unrelated genuine compromise, the operator should not improvise the next step solely on technical grounds. The engagement's established communication and escalation procedures determine what happens next.

The same discipline applies to credential material.

An identity assessment may expose passwords, hashes, Kerberos keys, private keys, authentication certificates, access tokens, refresh tokens, LAPS passwords, managed service account secrets, or other authentication artifacts. From a technical perspective, obtaining that material may demonstrate compromise. From an operational perspective, however, the assessor has now acquired sensitive authentication information whose storage, transmission, use, retention, and destruction must be governed.

This becomes particularly consequential when the material belongs to privileged identities, service accounts supporting mission systems, certificate authorities, federation services, synchronization platforms, or Tier 0 infrastructure. The fact that the credential was obtained during an authorized assessment does not make unrestricted subsequent use appropriate.

Good Rules of Engagement therefore constrain not only what the tester may attack, but what the tester may do with what is discovered.

Another important distinction is between authorization to demonstrate a path and authorization to traverse the entire path.

Suppose an assessor discovers that a low-privileged principal possesses control over a group, that the group controls a Group Policy Object, and that the GPO applies to Domain Controllers. The security consequence can often be established through evidence and controlled validation without actually deploying code to a production Domain Controller. Likewise, proving that a principal possesses directory replication rights may be sufficient to demonstrate DCSync exposure without extracting the entire domain credential database.

Professional offensive security is not measured by how far an operator can push every compromise.

It is measured by whether the operator can obtain sufficient evidence to establish the security consequence while remaining inside the authority granted for the engagement.

This is particularly important in federal environments, where the purpose of offensive testing is ultimately risk reduction and mission protection. The objective is not to "win" against the production environment. It is to identify where trust can be subverted, demonstrate that weakness to the level necessary to establish risk, preserve usable evidence, and enable defenders to correct the underlying condition.

Later offensive chapters will therefore distinguish between a technique's full technical capability and the level of execution appropriate for a particular assessment. Some procedures are suitable for direct laboratory reproduction but should ordinarily be demonstrated more conservatively against production identity infrastructure. Others require explicit coordination because their consequences can propagate across the identity control plane.

Rules of Engagement are what allow that distinction to exist before the operator reaches the keyboard.
