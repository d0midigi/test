# 1.6.4 Case Studies

Case studies serve a different purpose from laboratories. A lab isolates a mechanism so that it can be examined directly. Real incidents rarely provide that convenience.

Actual identity compromises develop through imperfect environments in which old decisions, organizational boundaries, administrative habits, legacy applications, incomplete monitoring, and operational pressure interact. The resulting compromise may not follow the clean progression shown in an instructional attack chain.

Case studies are therefore used to reconnect individual techniques to system-level failure.

Where the public record supports the necessary level of detail, incidents will be examined for the role identity played in the compromise rather than forcing every event into an Active Directory narrative. Some incidents are fundamentally credential failures. Others involve privilege, federation, remote access, service accounts, administrative tooling, or insufficient separation of control planes. Still others are useful because identity was not the initial failure but became the mechanism through which an adversary expanded control.

That distinction is important. Identity security should not become a theory that attempts to explain every cyber incident. Its value comes from accurately identifying where trust, authentication, authorization, and administrative authority materially changed the outcome.
