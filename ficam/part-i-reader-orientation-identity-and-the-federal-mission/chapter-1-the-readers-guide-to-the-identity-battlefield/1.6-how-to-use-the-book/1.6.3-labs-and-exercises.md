# 1.6.3 Labs and Exercises

The laboratories are intended to move the reader from recognition to verification.

A diagram can explain that a user possesses control over an object. A lab can require the reader to inspect the object's security descriptor and determine exactly which Access Control Entry creates that authority. Prose can explain Kerberoasting. A lab can require the reader to identify a service account, determine why the account is roastable, request the relevant ticket material, inspect the resulting evidence, remediate the account, and confirm that the original exposure has been removed.

The important part of a lab is therefore not merely obtaining the expected offensive result.

A complete exercise should leave the environment in a better-understood state than it began.

That means many exercises continue beyond exploitation. The reader may be asked to inspect logs, modify the insecure condition, rerun the original test, and determine whether the path has actually been closed. In other cases, the defensive objective may be primary: identify which telemetry is generated by a directory change, establish an audit condition, or compare expected administrative behavior with activity produced during adversarial emulation.

Where an exercise touches a mechanism capable of affecting broad portions of an environment, the lab will favor isolated infrastructure rather than encouraging unsafe replication against production systems. Active Directory has very few truly local mistakes. A change that appears confined to one object can propagate through replication, policy, authentication, or trust relationships much farther than expected.

The laboratory environment exists precisely so those consequences can be studied without learning them at the expense of a production mission.
