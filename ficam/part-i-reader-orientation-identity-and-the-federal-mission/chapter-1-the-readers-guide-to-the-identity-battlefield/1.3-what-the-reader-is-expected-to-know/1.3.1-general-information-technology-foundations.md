# 1.3.1 General Information Technology Foundations

Readers should understand the basic structure of an enterprise computing environment. They should be familiar with the idea that workstations, servers, applications, network devices, identity services, databases, and administrative systems communicate through interconnected networks and that those systems depend on one another in ways that are often invisible to end users.

At a minimum, concepts such as Internet Protocol (IP) addressing, hostnames, subnets, routing, firewalls, ports, Transmission Control Protocol (TCP), User Datagram Protocol (UDP), Domain Name System (DNS), client-server communication, and remote administration should not be entirely unfamiliar. The reader does not need the depth expected of a network engineer, but identity infrastructure cannot be understood as if it exists independently of the network carrying its authentication, directory, replication, certificate, federation, and management traffic.

For example, Kerberos depends heavily on DNS and time synchronization. Active Directory replication depends on network reachability and Remote Procedure Call (RPC). Lightweight Directory Access Protocol (LDAP) queries depend on connectivity to directory services. Certificate enrollment requires communication with certificate infrastructure. Federation depends on reachable identity providers, relying parties, signing infrastructure, and web protocols. Administrative movement frequently depends on Server Message Block (SMB), Windows Remote Management (WinRM), Remote Desktop Protocol (RDP), Windows Management Instrumentation (WMI), or related management interfaces.

A reader who understands the network only as "the thing underneath Active Directory" will eventually struggle to understand why identity attacks move the way they do.

For that reason, the networking material later in the book is intentionally identity-focused. It does not attempt to reproduce a general networking curriculum. Instead, it explains the specific transport, name-resolution, routing, segmentation, service-discovery, and authentication dependencies required to reason about identity security.

The central question is not merely, _"How does the network work?"_

It is, _"What must the network permit for the identity system to function, and what security consequences arise from those permitted communications?"_

That question becomes especially important in federal and military environments where identity traffic may cross multiple enclaves, security zones, mission networks, partner connections, and boundary-protection systems.
