# 1.3 What the Reader Is Expected to Know

Contested Terrain is written for a mixed technical audience, and that fact shapes how the material is presented. A federal Active Directory environment is rarely administered, assessed, defended, or authorized by a single discipline. Directory engineers, network engineers, identity architects, Information Systems Security Officers (ISSOs), Information Systems Security Managers (ISSMs), Security Control Assessors (SCAs), incident responders, threat hunters, red team operators, Public Key Infrastructure (PKI) administrators, cloud engineers, and mission owners may all interact with the same identity infrastructure while viewing it through very different professional lenses.

Accordingly, this book does not assume that every reader begins with identical knowledge. A penetration tester may already understand credential dumping and Kerberos abuse while having limited exposure to Federal Identity, Credential, and Access Management (FICAM). An ISSO may understand authorization packages, Plans of Action and Milestones (POA\&Ms), Security Technical Implementation Guides (STIGs), and continuous monitoring but may never have manually analyzed an Active Directory access control entry. A Windows administrator may understand Group Policy processing and replication topology but have little experience tracing privilege relationships from the perspective of an adversary. An Identity, Credential, and Access Management (ICAM) engineer may understand identity proofing, federation, and assurance requirements but have limited exposure to offensive Active Directory tradecraft.

Those differences are intentional parts of the audience rather than deficiencies to be corrected before reading.

The reader is expected to possess enough general technical familiarity to understand enterprise systems, networks, accounts, authentication, administration, and basic cybersecurity terminology. The reader is not expected to arrive knowing Active Directory internals, Kerberos message exchanges, X.509 certificate structures, Windows authorization internals, federation protocols, or offensive identity techniques. Those subjects are developed systematically before the book relies upon them.

This distinction is important because Contested Terrain is not structured as an Active Directory certification study guide, an introductory networking course, or a collection of penetration-testing recipes. Its purpose is to build an integrated identity-security model. Foundational subjects are therefore introduced to the depth required to understand their security consequences, then revisited from offensive, defensive, detection, governance, and recovery perspectives as the book progresses.

The reader should expect the difficulty to increase deliberately. Early chapters establish a common language. Architecture chapters explain how identity systems operate under normal conditions. Assessment chapters teach how to examine those systems responsibly. Offensive chapters demonstrate how adversaries exploit the resulting trust relationships. Defensive chapters then return to the same mechanisms and ask what must be engineered differently. Detection and recovery chapters assume the reader now understands both normal operation and adversarial abuse.

The expectation, therefore, is not prior mastery. It is cumulative learning.
