# 1.3.4 Basic Cybersecurity Concepts

The reader should understand the common cybersecurity vocabulary surrounding confidentiality, integrity, availability, least privilege, authentication, authorization, vulnerabilities, misconfigurations, logging, monitoring, incident response, threat actors, attack surfaces, privilege escalation, lateral movement, persistence, and defense in depth.

Those concepts will not simply be repeated. They will be refined for identity security.

Authentication, for example, answers whether a presented identity or authenticator is accepted as valid.

Authorization answers what the resulting principal is permitted to do.

Identity security must examine both, because successful authentication does not determine whether the resulting access is appropriate.

Likewise, privilege escalation is often described as a straightforward movement from a lower-privileged user to an administrator. Active Directory frequently behaves less cleanly.

A principal may possess no privileged group membership and still control an object that controls another object, which controls a group, which controls a Group Policy Object, which applies to a Domain Controller.

The original account has not necessarily been added to Domain Admins.

Its effective authority has nevertheless changed dramatically.

This is why the book repeatedly distinguishes visible privilege from effective control.

Traditional vulnerability analysis often asks:

"What vulnerabilities exist?"

Identity attack-path analysis must also ask:

"What relationships exist?"

"Who can modify whom?"

"Which credentials can authenticate where?"

"Which principals can change access control?"

"Which systems can manage other systems?"

"Which certificates can represent which identities?"

"Which synchronization or federation pathways extend authority into another security boundary?"

That relational view of security becomes increasingly important as the book progresses.
