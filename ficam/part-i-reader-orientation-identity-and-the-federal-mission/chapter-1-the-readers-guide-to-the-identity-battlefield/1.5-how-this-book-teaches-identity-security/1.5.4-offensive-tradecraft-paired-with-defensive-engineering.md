# 1.5.4 Offensive Tradecraft Paired With Defensive Engineering

The fourth rule is symmetry.

Every meaningful offensive technique should eventually force a defensive engineering question.

If the attacker discovers a pathway, the defender must understand how that pathway came to exist.

If the attacker abuses a permission, the defender must understand why the permission was assigned.

If the attacker steals a credential, the defender must understand where the credential was stored and why it was exposed.

If the attacker obtains a certificate, the defender must examine certificate issuance, mapping, template governance, and CA protection.

If the attacker traverses a trust, the defender must examine the trust's direction, scope, filtering, administrative ownership, and continued business or mission requirement.

This symmetry prevents the book from becoming two unrelated halves labeled "attack" and "defense."

The same object is examined from both sides.

Kerberoasting provides a useful example.

An offensive-only explanation might stop after obtaining and cracking a service-ticket hash.

A complete identity-security analysis continues.

Why does the service account possess an SPN?

Why is the account password old or predictable?

What privileges does the account possess?

Where can it log on?

Which applications depend on it?

Could the service migrate to a Group Managed Service Account (gMSA)?

What Kerberos encryption types are permitted?

What volume or pattern of service-ticket requests is normal?

How would defenders identify abnormal requests?

Would password rotation break the service?

Who owns the account?

When was it last reviewed?

What mission system depends upon it?

Only by answering those questions does the practitioner understand the full security problem.

Offense reveals the failure.

Defense must remove the condition that made the failure exploitable.
