# 1.5.2 Protocol Mechanics Before Protocol Abuse

The second rule is that the reader should understand the protocol before learning to abuse it.

Automation can conceal enormous amounts of security-relevant behavior.

A tool that appears to "perform DCSync" is actually interacting with directory-replication capabilities.

A tool that "Kerberoasts an account" is requesting a legitimate Kerberos service ticket and extracting material suitable for offline password recovery.

A relay tool does not magically bypass authentication. It receives or coerces an authentication exchange and attempts to present that exchange to another service under conditions where channel protection, signing, binding, or target restrictions do not prevent the relay.

The more abstraction a tool provides, the easier it becomes to misunderstand the security condition.

Protocol knowledge allows the practitioner to answer more useful questions.

Why did the attack succeed against LDAP but fail against LDAPS?

Why did SMB relay fail when signing was required?

Why did a Kerberos request select a particular encryption type?

Why did a certificate authenticate successfully for one account but not another?

Why does DCSync require specific replication rights rather than Domain Admin membership itself?

Why does time synchronization matter to authentication?

Why does a service principal need an SPN?

Why does channel binding alter relay feasibility?

Those questions cannot be answered by memorizing commands.

The book therefore repeatedly descends beneath the tooling layer and examines the underlying exchanges, objects, rights, trust decisions, and security controls.

The goal is not to convert every reader into a protocol developer.

The goal is to ensure the reader understands enough mechanics to reason about security outcomes.
