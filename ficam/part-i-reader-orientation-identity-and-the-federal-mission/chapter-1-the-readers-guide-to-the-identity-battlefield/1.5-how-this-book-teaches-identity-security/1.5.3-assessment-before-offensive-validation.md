# 1.5.3 Assessment Before Offensive Validation

The third rule is that offensive validation must occur inside a professional assessment methodology.

A technically capable operator can cause enormous damage while performing an otherwise legitimate security test.

This risk becomes particularly serious in identity infrastructure because centralized identity systems possess broad operational reach.

A single Group Policy modification can propagate across hundreds or thousands of endpoints.

A certificate revocation can affect authentication across multiple applications.

A Domain Controller failure can disrupt authentication and directory services.

A trust modification can affect cross-domain or mission-partner access.

A synchronization change can propagate identities or attributes into cloud services.

A poorly planned KRBTGT reset can affect Kerberos authentication across the domain.

An offensive security practitioner therefore needs more than technical skill.

They need operational judgment.

Before executing an identity-security technique, the operator should understand scope, authorization, expected effect, possible secondary effect, restoration procedure, evidence requirements, and abort conditions.

The operator should know whether the target is production.

They should know whether the identity controls a mission-essential service.

They should know whether the account is used by automation.

They should know whether a certificate is shared.

They should know whether an action may cross an authorization boundary.

They should know whether mission partners could be affected.

This is why the book places professional assessment methodology before the offensive lifecycle.

The objective is not to weaken offensive realism.

It is to ensure that realistic testing does not become uncontrolled experimentation.
