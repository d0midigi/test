# 3.5.4 Tactical and Disconnected Environments

Disconnected, intermittent, and low-bandwidth environments expose the tension between centralized identity and operational autonomy most clearly.

Central identity is attractive because it creates consistency. Authoritative accounts, common credentials, centralized policy, revocation, auditing, and entitlement governance become easier when systems can communicate reliably with enterprise infrastructure.

Tactical environments cannot always assume that connectivity.

A system may have to continue operating while disconnected from central identity services. Authentication may have to rely on cached information, locally available certificate validation data, replicated directory state, or other mechanisms capable of functioning independently for a period of time.

The engineering problem is difficult because security freshness and operational availability pull in different directions.

A disconnected system cannot continuously consult an authoritative source for the newest account state.

It may not immediately learn that a credential has been revoked.

It may not receive the newest Group Policy configuration.

It may not be able to reach central telemetry platforms.

Yet denying all access when connectivity disappears may be operationally unacceptable.

The resulting architecture must define how much trust can be carried forward during isolation and for how long.

This is not merely a networking problem.

It is a question of identity validity under degraded conditions.

A resilient design must know which identity information can safely remain cached, how stale state is reconciled when connectivity returns, what privileges remain available during disconnection, and what evidence is preserved locally for later review.

For mission systems, continuity is not achieved by eliminating identity controls when communications degrade.

It is achieved by designing identity controls that degrade intentionally.
