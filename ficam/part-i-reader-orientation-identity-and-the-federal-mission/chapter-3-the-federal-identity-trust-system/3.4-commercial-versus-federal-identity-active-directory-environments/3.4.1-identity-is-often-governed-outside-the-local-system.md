# 3.4.1 Identity Is Often Governed Outside the Local System

One of the most important differences is that the local Active Directory administrator may not be the ultimate authority over the identity represented by an account.

A user may originate in a personnel or workforce system. Sponsorship may occur through a separate process. Credential issuance may occur through infrastructure not controlled by the local directory team. Attributes may be derived from enterprise authoritative sources. Access may require approvals from data owners or mission owners outside the identity organization.

Active Directory then consumes or represents portions of that identity state.

This means the local account is not necessarily the beginning of the identity lifecycle.

Nor is deleting the account necessarily the entirety of deprovisioning.

A federal identity may exist simultaneously across physical access systems, logical access systems, cloud services, enterprise applications, PKI, mission-partner environments, and locally maintained directories. Different portions of the lifecycle may therefore be controlled by different authorities.

This distribution creates legitimate governance complexity, but it also creates security dependencies.

If an upstream source incorrectly indicates that a person remains eligible for access, downstream provisioning may preserve identities that should have been removed. If an attribute changes incorrectly, entitlements driven by that attribute may also change. If local deprovisioning occurs without corresponding enterprise revocation, another valid path may remain.

Identity security must therefore account for lifecycle state across the trust system rather than treating account enablement as the complete measure of access.
