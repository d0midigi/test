# Copy of 6.1 Federal Identity, Credential, and Access Management

## 5.1 How Federal Cybersecurity Authority Reaches the Technology

### 5.1.1 Governance Is a Chain of Authority

{% hint style="info" %}
Federal cybersecurity requirements do not originate from a single document. Statutes, executive direction, agency policy, Department of Defense issuances, federal standards, control frameworks, technical implementation guidance, and local procedures collectively shape how identity systems are designed and secured.
{% endhint %}

### 5.1.2 Authority Becomes More Specific as It Approaches Implementation

{% hint style="info" %}
High-level law may establish an obligation to protect federal information, while policy, standards, controls, implementation guidance, and technical baselines progressively translate that obligation into enforceable system behavior.
{% endhint %}

### 5.1.3 The Same Technical Control May Serve Multiple Governance Purposes

{% hint style="info" %}
A configuration such as multifactor authentication, privileged-access restriction, certificate protection, account recertification, or audit logging may simultaneously satisfy identity, cybersecurity, privacy, Zero Trust, Risk Management Framework, and mission-assurance requirements.
{% endhint %}

### 5.1.4 Governance Must Remain Traceable to Real Configuration

{% hint style="info" %}
A requirement has little defensive value if practitioners cannot connect it to a technical object, configuration state, operational process, log source, or measurable behavior.
{% endhint %}

### 5.1.5 Compliance Evidence and Security Evidence Are Related but Not Identical

{% hint style="info" %}
A completed checklist or control statement may demonstrate that a requirement was addressed administratively, but technical evidence is required to establish whether the implemented control actually functions as intended.
{% endhint %}
