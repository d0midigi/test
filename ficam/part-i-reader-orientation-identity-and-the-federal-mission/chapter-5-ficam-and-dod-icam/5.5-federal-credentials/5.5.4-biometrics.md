# 5.5.4 Biometrics

Biometrics occupy a more nuanced role in federal identity than the common description of “something you are” suggests.

A biometric characteristic can support identity proofing, verification, authenticator activation, or other identity functions depending on the architecture. Its security properties therefore depend heavily on where in the lifecycle it is used and what other controls surround it.

Biometric data differs fundamentally from a password.

A password can be changed when compromised.

A person's fingerprint, face, or iris cannot be replaced in the same manner.

This makes biometric template protection, matching architecture, presentation-attack resistance, fallback mechanisms, and lifecycle controls important components of biometric security.

Biometrics also should not be interpreted as inherently establishing identity without context.

A biometric comparison establishes a relationship between a presented biometric sample and a reference or enrolled biometric under the parameters of the matching system. The security meaning depends on how the reference was established and how strongly the system protects against impersonation, replay, substitution, and inappropriate fallback.

FIPS 201-3 incorporates biometric mechanisms into the PIV system and updated PIV requirements to support modern proofing and credentialing processes. ([3](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/FIPS/NIST.FIPS.201-3.pdf))

For Active Directory security, biometrics should therefore be understood primarily as part of the broader authenticator and identity-proofing architecture rather than as an independent directory authentication protocol.

Windows Hello for Business provides a useful example of why the distinction matters. A user may activate a device-bound cryptographic credential through a biometric gesture. The biometric itself is not necessarily what the Domain Controller receives and validates as the domain authentication credential. The biometric can be used locally to unlock or authorize use of protected key material, after which the cryptographic protocol establishes the remote identity.

This design is substantially different from sending a fingerprint through the network as though it were a password.

The security model is consequently determined by the credential architecture surrounding the biometric.
