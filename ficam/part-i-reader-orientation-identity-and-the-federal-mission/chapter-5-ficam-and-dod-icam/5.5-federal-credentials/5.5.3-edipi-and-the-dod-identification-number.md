# 5.5.3 EDIPI and the DoD Identification Number

The Electronic Data Interchange Personal Identifier (EDI-PI), commonly encountered as EDIPI, is the identifier that became the DoD Identification Number. DoD policy describes the DoD Identification Number as the common name for the EDI-PI and establishes it as a unique identifier associated with individuals in the DoD identity environment. ([5](https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodi/832003p.pdf))

Its security significance is frequently misunderstood because identifiers and authenticators are easily conflated.

An EDIPI identifies.

It does not authenticate by itself.

Knowledge of a person's EDIPI should not be treated as proof that the person presenting the value is the individual represented by it. The same principle applies to other enterprise identifiers. A stable identifier is useful precisely because it can consistently associate records representing the same subject across systems. That usefulness does not turn the identifier into a secret.

This distinction matters when Active Directory, applications, certificate mappings, and authoritative personnel systems exchange identity data.

A name is a poor enterprise identifier because names change and are not necessarily unique. Email addresses can change. Usernames are implementation-specific and may differ between environments. A stable enterprise identifier can provide a more reliable correlation point between identity records.

The danger appears when a relying system treats possession of the identifier as if it establishes identity.

Identifiers support correlation.

Authenticators establish proof of control.

Authorization determines resulting access.

Those three functions should not be collapsed simply because one field appears in several of them.

#### Identifier Integrity and Identity Correlation

The fact that an identifier is not an authenticator does not mean its integrity is unimportant.

If an application uses EDIPI to correlate a CAC-authenticated subject with an internal account, incorrect mapping can associate legitimate authentication with the wrong authorization record. If provisioning processes use the identifier to correlate records across systems, erroneous or duplicate associations can propagate incorrect identity state.

The attack surface therefore exists less in learning the identifier than in manipulating the systems that interpret it.

This pattern occurs repeatedly in identity security. The most consequential weakness may not exist in the authentication protocol itself. It may exist in the mapping layer that tells the relying system which internal principal the authenticated external identity represents.

Certificate-to-account mapping, federation subject mapping, synchronization correlation, and authoritative identifier mapping all share this characteristic.

Correct authentication followed by incorrect identity correlation can still produce unauthorized access.
