# 5.1.4 Federation Extends Trust Without Transferring Ownership

Federation solves a difficult enterprise identity problem: how to accept another authority's identity without becoming responsible for managing that identity directly.

The FICAM Architecture defines federation as the set of technologies, policies, standards, and processes that allows an agency to accept identities, attributes, and credentials managed elsewhere. NIST SP 800-63C-4 similarly treats federation as a relationship in which a credential service provider or identity provider conveys authentication information and potentially subscriber attributes to separately administered relying parties. (\[1])

This enables interagency and mission-partner access without requiring every external user to become a fully locally managed identity.

But the relying party still owns the local authorization decision.

Federation does not mean that Agency A gets to decide what its users may do inside Agency B's application.

Agency A may establish and authenticate the identity.

Agency B decides what access its own resource grants to that identity.

That separation is one of the most important controls in federated identity.

The upstream authority answers who the user is and, depending on the architecture, supplies trusted attributes about that user.

The downstream authority decides what those facts mean locally.

When those responsibilities become blurred, federation can become an uncontrolled transference of privilege rather than controlled transference of identity trust.
