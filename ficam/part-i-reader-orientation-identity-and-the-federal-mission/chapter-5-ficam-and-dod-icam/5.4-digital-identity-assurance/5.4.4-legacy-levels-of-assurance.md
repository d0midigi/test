# 5.4.4 Legacy Levels of Assurance

Older federal identity architectures often described identity confidence through a single Level of Assurance model.

That model is historically important because many legacy system documents, architecture descriptions, contracts, and application requirements still use its terminology.

The modern NIST model deliberately decomposes assurance into IAL, AAL, and FAL instead.

That decomposition is a substantial improvement for security analysis because it prevents one number from obscuring several different properties.

A system may require strong confidence in the identity but support relatively ordinary authentication.

Another may require strong phishing-resistant authentication without requiring the highest identity-proofing level.

A federated service may require protections against assertion theft or injection beyond what is implied by the user's authentication strength alone.

These are different risks.

Treating them separately allows the architecture to select assurance according to the transaction rather than assuming one universal identity-strength value follows the user everywhere.

That change also aligns better with offensive analysis.

An adversary does not experience “assurance” as one property.

They look for whichever component is weakest.

If proofing is strong, they target credential theft.

If authentication is strong, they may target authenticator registration or account recovery.

If both are strong, they may target federation tokens.

If federation is well protected, they may target downstream authorization.

The assurance chain is only as effective as the implementation that connects its parts.
