# 5.11.3 NPE Credentials Behave Differently From Human Credentials

A human user can enter a password, insert a smart card, provide a PIN, interact with a phishing-resistant authenticator, or complete another interactive ceremony.

Many NPEs cannot.

Services and workloads need authentication mechanisms that operate without constant human participation.

Historically, this often led to long-lived passwords stored in service configurations, scripts, registry values, scheduled tasks, application settings, or deployment systems.

Those credentials were operationally convenient because they did not change unexpectedly.

They were also dangerous for exactly the same reason.

A password that never changes becomes increasingly valuable once exposed.

Managed service accounts, Group Managed Service Accounts (gMSAs), delegated Managed Service Accounts (dMSAs), workload identities, managed identities, certificate-based authentication, and other modern mechanisms attempt to improve this model by reducing dependence on manually maintained static secrets.

The technical solution varies.

The security objective is consistent: the workload should receive enough authentication capability to perform its function without creating an unnecessarily reusable secret that administrators, applications, attackers, and backup systems can all collect.

NPE credential design is therefore inseparable from secret-management architecture.
