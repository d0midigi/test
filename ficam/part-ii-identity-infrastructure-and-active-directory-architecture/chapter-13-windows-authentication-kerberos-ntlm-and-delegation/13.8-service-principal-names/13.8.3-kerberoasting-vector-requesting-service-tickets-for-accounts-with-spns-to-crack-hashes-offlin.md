# 13.8.3 Kerberoasting Vector: Requesting Service Tickets for Accounts with SPNs to Crack Hashes Offline

### Introduction

This chapter section provides the technical foundation for understanding, executing in an assessment, and defending against Kerberoasting - the abuse of the Kerberos Ticket Granting Service (TGS) to obtain crackable credential material for any account carrying a Service Principal Name (SPN). It positions Kerberoasting within the credential acquisition phase of the Active Directory Identity Attack Lifecycle and equips readers with the enumeration methodology, tooling, offline cracking workflow, and defensive architecture required to assess and harden federal and military identity environments against this malicious technique.

Kerberoasting exploits a deliberate design property of the Kerberos v5 authentication protocol rather than exploit a software bug or flaw: any authenticated domain account may request a service ticket for any registered Service Principal Name (SPN), and that ticket is encrypted using a key derived from the target service account's password. Where the encryption relies on RC4, or where the underlying password is weak, an adversary can extract the ticket and recover the plaintext password through offline brute-forcing techniques, entirely outside the purview and visibility of domain-wide and enforced account lockout policy. This section walks through the Kerberos protocol's mechanics that make this technique more than possible, the enumeration and extraction methodology using both native Windows facilities and purpose-built offensive tooling, the offline cracking economics that separate RC4 from AES-protected accounts, and the defensive architecture - managed service accounts, encryption enforcement, and detection engineering - that closes the exposure pathway. Federal-specific considerations, including the common misconception that smart card authentication neutralizes this technique, are addressed directly.

#### Learning Objectives

Upon completion, readers will be able to:

* Explain why the Kerberos protocol permits any authenticated user to request a service ticket for any registered Service Principal Name (SPN).
* Enumerate Kerberoastable accounts using native Windows tools, PowerView, Impacket, and Rubeus.
* Request, extract, and format service tickets for offline cracking with `hashcat` and John the Ripper (JtR).
* Distinguish RC4-protected assets from AES-protected tickets and explain the cracking-cost differential between the two.
* Design a defensive architecture - managed service accounts, encryption-type enforcement, and privilege containment - that closes the Kerberoasting attack surface.
* Build detection logic specifically against Windows Event ID 4769 that distinguishes malicious ticket-request sweeps from normal service activities.
* Evaluate a federal identity environment's exposure to Kerberoasting independent of its smart card authentication posture.

### Kerberoasting Within the Credential Acquisition (Post-Enumeration and Data Harvesting) Phase

Kerberoasting occupies a specific and consistent position in the identity attack lifecycle: it is a post-enumeration, pre-lateral-movement technique that converts directory visibility into usable credential material. By the time you reach this point in an assessment, you should have already completed a couple of thorough enumeration sweeps and performed an analysis of the harvested data - you know which accounts exist, which of them carry a Service Principal Name (SPN), and which are worth prioritizing. Kerberoasting is the mechanism that turns that knowledge into a usable password.

#### _The Attack in One Sentence_

Kerberoasting is the practice of requesting a Kerberos service ticket for any account that has a legitimately registered Service Principal Name (SPN) issued via Active Dir3ectory Domain Services (AD DS) upon account provisioning, extracting the encrypted portion of that ticket, and attempting to recover the underlying password offline (e.g., not done within the target environment), because the ticket is encrypted with a key derived directly from the target account's password.

#### _Where It Sits in the Identity Attack Lifecycle_

Kerberoasting requires only a valid, authenticated domain session - it does not require local administrative rights on any system, and it does not require the targeted service account to be logged on anywhere. Any domain-joined user with a valid Ticket Granting Ticket (TGT) can request a service ticket for any Service Principal Name (SPN) in the forest, which is precisely what makes this technique reachable from the lowest rung of access: a single compromised standard user account, obtained through either phishing, spearphishing, password spraying, or a helpdesk-reset abuse chain sufficient enough to begin the Kerberoasting attack.

#### _Why Service Accounts Are the Target of Choice_

Computer accounts also carry Service Principal Names and are, in principle, Kerberoastable. In practice, they are rarely worth the time and effort: computer account passwords are 120-character random values generated and rotated automatically, and no cracking wordlist, dictionary, or GPU cluster will recover them within a realistic timeframe. Human-managed service accounts are an entirely different story. They are frequently provisioned once, during a project, with a password chosen by a person under time pressure, and then left untouched for years. That gap between a machine-generated secret and a human-generated one is the entire economic basis for this attack, and it is worth internalizing before the mechanics that follow, because every defensive recommendation will trace back to closing the specific gap mentioned above.

### Kerberos Ticketing Mechanics Relevant to the Kerberoasting Attack

A full treatment of Kerberos authentication appears in Chapter 3. This section revisits only the portion of the protocol that Kerberoasting truly depends on: the Ticket Granting Service (TGS) exchange and the encryption key that protects its response.

#### _The AS Exchange and the TGT_

_Authentication_ begins with the _Authentication Service (AS)_ exchange, in which a legitimate3 user proves knowledge of a secret - a password hash under standard authentication, or a certificate-bound key under Personal Identity Verification (PIV) or Common Access Card (CAC) smart card logon - to the Key Distribution Center (KDC), and receives a Ticket Granting Ticket (TGT) in return. The TGT is encrypted with a key derived from the `krbtgt` account and is not, by itself, useful for Kerberoasting. Its role here is simply to prove that the requester is an authenticated principal within the domain.

#### _The TGS Exchange and the Service Ticket (ST)_

Holding a valid TGT, a client requests a service ticket from the Ticket Granting Service (TGS) by sending a TGS-REQ (TGS Request) that names the Service Principal Name (SPN) of the resource it wants, or is trying to reach. The KDC responds with a TGS-REP (TGS Response) containing a service ticket. Critically, the encrypted portion of that ticket - the part that authorizes the holder to present it to the target service - is encrypted using a key derived from the target service account's own password, not from anything belonging to the requester. The KDC performs this encryption on request, for any SPN that exists in the directory, without validating whether the requester has any legitimate business with that service. Authorization is the target service's problem, evaluated later, against the _Privilege Attribute Certificate (PAC)_ embedded in the ticket. Ticket issuance itself is not an authorization decision.

#### _Ticket Encryption Types and the `msDS-SupportedEncrytionTypes` Attribute_

The key used to encrypt the service ticket highly depends on which encryption types the target account supports, recorded in its `msDS-SupportedEncryptionTypes` attribute. An account still permitted to use RC4-HMAC will produce a ticket encrypted with a key that is functionally the account's NT hash - a single, unsalted MD4 (Message Digest 4) digest of the password, and a correspondingly fast target for a GPU-based cracking attempt. An account that is restricted to AES128 or AES256 will produce a ticket protect4ed by a key derived through PBKDF2 with four thousand and ninety-six (4.096) iterations and a domain-specific salt, which is dramatically more expensive to attack per candidate password. Table 13-1 below summarizes the practical differences.

| Encryption Type         | E-type Value | Key Derivation                                 | Hashcat Mode | Relative Cracking Cost                                         |
| ----------------------- | ------------ | ---------------------------------------------- | ------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| RC4-HMAC                | 23 (`0x17`)  | Single MD4 pass over the password (NT hash)    | 13100        | Low - billions of candidates per second on modern GPU hardware |
| AES128-CTS-HMAC-SHA1-96 | 17 (`0x11`)  | PBKDF2, 4,096 iterations, domain-specific salt | 19600        | High - several orders of magnitude slower per candidate        |
| AES256-CTS-HMAC-SHA1-96 | 18 (`0x12`)  | PBKDF2, 4,096 iterations, domain-specific salt | 19700        | High - several orders of magnitude slower per candidate        |

#### _Why Any Authenticated User Can Request Any Service Ticket_

It is worth noting and worth stating plainly, because it is the singe-most common misconception federal practitioners bring to this topic: this behavior is not a misconfiguration. It is legitimately how Kerberos was originally designed and intended to work, and changing it would break the protocol's entire trust model. The defensive answer is therefore never to _"prevent the request"_ - that request is legitimate protocol behavior and cannot be blocked without absolutely breaking all functionality of Kerberos authentication, generally speaking. The defensive answer is to make the material returned by that request worthless to an attacker, either by making it uncrackable in practice or by removing the human-chosen password it would otherwise expose.

### Identifying Kerberoastable Accounts

Extraction depends on enumeration. It is the only way; otherwise how would an attacker know exactly what's worth extracting without having done prior reconnaissance of their target of interest? Before a single ticket is even requested, an assessor - or equally, an adversary - first needs a list of accounts that are known precisely to be carrying an SPN, and ideally ranked by how likely each one is to be both privileged yet poorly protected.

#### _The Service Principal Name (SPN) Attribute_

A Service Principal Name (SPN) is an Active Directory attribute, specifically `servicePrincipalName`, that binds a services' instance to an account under whose security context it is running. It takes the general form `service-class/host:port`, as in `MSSQLSvc/sql01.contoso.mil:1433`. Any account - user- or computer-based - preferably with a non-empty `servicePrincipalName` attribute is, by definition, Kerberoastable.

#### _Lightweight Directory Access Protocol (LDAP)-Based SPN Enumeration_

The most portable enumeration (or, "enum" for short) method is a direct LDAP-based query against Active Directory, filtering for user objects with a populated `servicePrincipalName` attribute.

```
(&(objectCategory=user)(servicePrincipalName=*))
```

This filter deliberately excludes computer accounts, which _also_ carry SPNs; however, they are considered to be impractical cracking targets because their passwords are automatically generated, extremely complex (typically 120-characters as mentioned above), and rotate every 30 days, making brute-force or offline cracking attempts computationally impossible (or unrealistic). `ldapsearch`, PowerShell's `ADSISearcher`, or a graphical tool such as Softerra LDAP Administrator - can execute this query against a domain controller using nothing more than an authenticated, unprivileged domain account.

When performing Service Principal Name (SPN) enumeration, the goal is to find accounts running services that can be targeted for offline password cracking. Adversaries intentionally use the filter as demonstrated above to filter out computer accounts (like `MACHINENAME$`) for the following reasons:

* **No Human Footprint for Lateral Movement:** Computer accounts do not have human-engineered password reuse vulnerabilities. Even if an attacker compromised a computer account's credentials, those credentials cannot be used to log into other enterprise web portals, check target email, or socially engineer other users, severely limiting an attacker's post-exploitation value and significance.
* **The "`SYSTEM`" Privilege Dead-End:** Cracking a standard service account often grants direct access to specific, sensitive database applications or file shares. Conversely, a computer account primarily grants local system permissions on that specific host - access that an attacker usually already possesses if they are running the enumeration tools from that machine in the first place.
* **Elevated Detection Risks:** Computer accounts interact with Domain Controllers constantly for background system processes. Attempting to manipulate, authenticate, with, or lock out a machine account via anomalous Kerberos requests triggers immediate, high-fidelity security alerts in modern Endpoint Detection and Response (EDR) and Security Information and Event Management (SIEM) systems, whereas user account anomalies can more easily blend into normal employee behavior.

#### _The Randomly Generated 120-Character Password_

When we say "Active Directory automatically assigns" these properties, the heavy lifting is handled by a combination of the **Netlogon Service**, the **Active Directory Schema**, and the **Domain Controller (DC)**. Here is exactly how it works under the hood:

```
[ Client Computer ]                                   [ Domain Controller ]
  (Netlogon Service)                                   (Active Directory)
          │                                                    │
          │ 1. Triggers every 30 days via Netlogon registry     │
          ├───────────────────────────────────────────────────>│
          │                                                    │
          │ 2. DC generates random, cryptographically secure    │
          │    120-character password string                   │
          │<───────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
          │                                                    │
          │ 3. Locally stores password in LSA Secrets          │
          │    Updates machine account attribute in AD         │
          ▼                                                    ▼
```

#### _The Engine: The `Netlogon` Service_

The automation is driven on the endpoint by the Netlogon service (`netlogon.dll`). This background Windows service runs constantly on every domain-joined machine.

#### _The Trigger: Registry Timers_

Inside the Windows Registry of the computer (`HKLM\System\CurrentControlSet\Services\Netlogon\Parameters`), there is a specific directive called `MaximumPasswordAge`. By default, this is set to 30 days. When this internal timer expires, the `Netlogon` service automatically reaches out to the nearest Domain Controller to request a password change.

#### _The Generator: The Domain Controller_

The local computer does not type out a password. Instead, the Domain Controller uses its internal cryptographic random number generator to create a highly complex, 120-character unreadable string.

#### _The Storage: LSA Secrets & AD Attributes_

Once generated:

1. The Domain Controller updates the machine's password history in the Active Directory database.
2. The local computer securely stores this password in an encrypted, protected memory space known as **LSA Secrets** (Local Security Authority).

Because this entire loop happens invisibly in the background between two machine services, no human ever sees, knows, or interacts with the computer account password.

#### PowerView and Native Domain Enumeration

PowerView wraps the same underlying query into a purpose-built cmdlet that additionally surfaces the account's privilege indicators:

```
Get-DomainUser -SPN -Properties samaccountname,serviceprincipalname,pwdlastset,memberof | Format-Table -AutoSize
```

The native Windows equivalent, `setspn.exe`, ships with all versions of Windows Server and requires no additional tooling:

```
setspn.exe -T contoso.mil -Q */*
```

Because `setspn.exe` is a signed, built-in Microsoft binary, its use blends into normal administrative activities far more effectively than dropping PowerView or a compiled offensive tool onto a host, which matters directly for the operational security (OPSEC) discussion in the following sections.

#### Prioritizing Targets: AdminCount, Group Membership, Password Age (pwdLastSet)

A directory of any size can return dozens or even hundreds of Kerberoastable accounts depending on the size of domain, and not all of them are worth pursuing. There are three key attributes that can do most of the prioritization work. The `adminCount` attribute, set to `1` on accounts that have ever been a member of a protected group such as Domain Admins, is a strong signal of privilege even where current group membership has since been trimmed. Current **group membership** in domain privileged groups such as Domain Admins, Enterprise Admins, Schema Admins, or even a locally-scoped administrative group is a stronger signal still. And `pwdLastSet`, when it can be read, tells you how long the account's password has had to drift from whatever rotation policy the organization believes is in force - an account whose password was last set nine years ago, as described in the field account of a service account audit is exactly the kind of target that makes Kerberoasting a practical rather than theoretical threat.

#### Offensive Execution: Requesting and Extracting Service Tickets (ST)

With a prioritized list in hand, extraction proceeds through one of several tool pathways, ranging from a native-only technique that uses no offensive tooling at all to fully automated frameworks that enumerate, request, and format output for cracking in a single command.

```
Add-Type -AssemblyName System.IdentityModel
New-Object System.IdentityModel.Tokens.KerberosRequestorSecurityToken
-ArgumentList "MSSQLSvc/sql01.contoso.mil:1433"
```

The commands above forces a `TGS-REQ/TGS-REP` exchange for that SPN and stores the resulting ticket in the current session's Kerberos ticket cache, visible by executing the command `klist`. The ticket must then be exported from memory and this is where the tool Mimikatz most excels. Mimikatz's `kerberos::list /export` dumps every cached ticket into a `.kirbi` file on disk, and a converting tool such as `kirbi2john.py` reformats into a hash that John the Ripper (JtR) or `hashcat` can consume. The method requires no third-party executable beyond a small Python converter, at the cost of being slower and more manual than the purpose-built tools that follow.

#### Rubeus

Rubeus, part of the GhostPack tolset, automates the entire workflow - SPN enumeration, ticket request, extraction, and hashcat-ready formatting - in a single invocation as such:

```
Rubeus.exe kerberoast /outfile:roast_hashes.txt
```

Two flags matter for a disciplined assessment.

First, the `/rc4opsec` flag restricts requests to accounts that still support legacy RC4, avoiding tickets that Rubeus would otherwise request in AES and that offer little practical cracking value, which reduces the volume of TGS-REQ traffic generated against the domain controller(s). Second, the `/user` flag narrows execution to a single named account once a high-value target has already been identified through the prioritization step in **SECTION** rather than sweeping the entire directory, which is often noisy and can easily trigger defensive unauthorized network scanning alerting mechanisms.

```
Rubeus.exe kerberoast /user:svc_sqlreporting /outfile:target_hash.txt /rc4opsec
```

#### Impacket's GetUserSPNs.py

For engagements conducted from a Linux-based assessment platform rather than a Windows host inside the target environment, Impackets `GetUserSPNs.py` performs the same enumeration-and-request workflow over the domain network, authentiating directly agains the targeted domain controller as such:

```
GetUserrSPNs.py contoso.mil/y.silva:'P@ssw0rd123' -dc-ip 10.20.30.5 -request \ -outputfile roast_hashes.txt
```

Here, the `-request` flag is what ultimately distinguishes an enumeration pass from a full extraction; omitting it lists Kerberoastable accounts without having to request for tickets, which is useful for the reconnaissance pass previously conducted before committing to the noisier request phase.

#### PowerSploit's Invoke-Kerberoast

PowerView's `Invoke-Kerberoast` cmdlet offers a PowerShell-native equivalent to Rubeus, useful in environments where a compiled .NET binary is more likely to trigger endpoint detection than a PowerShell module already present through Living-Off-The-Land (LOTL) tooling alone:

```
Invoke-Kerberoast -Identity svc_sqlreporting -OutputFormat Hashcat | Select-Object -ExpandProperty Hash | Out-File roast_hashes.txt
```

#### OPSEC Considerations for Ticket Requests

Every TGS-REQ generated during enumeration or extraction phases produces a corresponding Windows Event ID **4769** on the issuing domain controller. A sweep that requests tickets for every SPN in the directory in a short window is fast indeed, but it is also the single most loudest, most detectable pattern this section covers. A disciplined assessment - and a disciplined adversary - narrows the request list to previously identified high-value targets, spaces requests over time where the engagement allows it, and prefers `/rc4opsec`-style filtering over a blind noisy sweep. None of this defeats detection, that is built correctly; however, it simply reflects that offensive tradecraft and detection engineering are answering the same question from opposite sides.

Table 13-2 below demonstrates offensive tooling for Kerberoasting.

| Tool                          | Platform                              | Requires Elevated Privileges | Output                                               |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------- | ---------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| .NET / Mimikatz               | Windows, native + Mimikatz            | No                           | Cached ticket, requires manual export and conversion |
| Rubeus                        | Windows, compiled .NET                | No                           | Hashcat-ready hash, direct to file                   |
| Impacket `GetUserSPNs.py`     | Linux / cross-platform, network-based | No                           | Hashcat-ready hash, direct to file                   |
| PowerView `Invoke-Kerberoast` | Windows, PowerShell                   | No                           | Hashcat-ready hash via pipeline                      |

### Offline Hash Cracking

It is important to note that extraction produces a hash, not a password. The final offensive step is an offline brute-force or dictionary-based attack against that very hash, conducted entirely outside the target environment and therefore invisible and undetectable to any defensive control that depends on observing authentication attempts.

#### Hash Formats and Cracking Tools

Both `hashcat` and John the Ripper (JtR) accept the `$krb5tgs$` hash format that Rubeus, Impacket, and PowerView all produce natively. The two tools differ mainly in performance characteristics and rule-based mutation support, and most practitioners keep both readily available.

#### hashcat Syntax and Mode Selection

For an RC4-protected ticket, hashcat mode `13100` selects the correct hash algorithm, and a rule file extends a base wordlist with common human password mutations:

```
hashcat -m 13100 roast_hashes.txt rockyou.txt -r rules/best64.rule
```

For AES-protected tickets, hashcat modes `19600` and `19700` select the AES128 and AES256 variants respectively. Cracking speed against these modes is dramatically lower than against mode `13100`. The reason for this is because hashcat mode `13100` targets Kerberos 5 TGS-REP etype 23 (RC4-HMAC), which uses a weak stream cipher and an obsolete MD4-based checksum that hashcat can process at extreme speeds. Modes `19600` and `19700` target AES128 and AES256 encrypted tickets (Kerberos etypes 17 and 18), which require heavy HMAC-SHA1 key derivation loops and secure AES block cipher operations per candidate.

The vast speed difference comes from the cryptographic design of the underlying algorithms. RC4 and MD4 involve basic bitwise math and simple permutations that map very efficiently to GPU hardware. In contrast, AES demands complex substitution-permutation networks, and Kerberos AES encryption wraps these in strict key derivation functions that force the processor to perform multiple resource-heavy and intensive rounds of hashing for every single password guess.

```
hashcat -m 19700 roast_hashes_aes.txt rockyou.txt -r rules/best64.rule
```

#### _John the Ripper (JtR) Extraction Syntax_

John the Ripper's `krb5tgs` format performs the equivalent attack:

```
john --format=krb5tgs --wordlist=rockyou.txt roast_hashes.txt
```

John auto-detects the specific hash format from the `$krb5tgs$` string itself, so the same invocation handles both RC4 and AES variants without a separate mode flag the way `hashcat` requires. Recovered passwords do not print to the console automatically during a run; the `--show` flag against the same hash file after a job completes, or after a job is interrupted and resumed, lists every candidate cracked so far:

```
john --show roast_hashes.txt
```

Because Kerberoasting is an attack where any authenticated domain user requests a Kerberos Ticket Granting Service (TGS) ticket for any service running with a domain user account as its service principal name, the domain controller will encrypt a portion of this TGS ticket using the target service account's password hash, derived from its NTLM or AES key. Because this ticket is delivered to the client user regardless of privilege level, an attacker can extract the encrypted portion offline through manual brute-forcing techniques.

The `$krb5tgs$` format in the John the Ripper targets this specific cryptographic structure inside the requested ticket. The tool takes the extracted hash from the `roast_hashes.txt` file and attempts to decrypt the service ticket validation block. It processes this by hashing each candidate word from the provided wordlist, such as `rockyou.txt`, using the appropriate derivation function for the Kerberos encryption type (like RC4 HMAC or AES).

If a candidate word's derived hash successfully decrypts the ticket's validation component, John the Ripper declares a match and will reveal the plaintext password of the targeted service account. Because service accounts often carry weaker or older passwords and high privileges like Domain Admins, cracking this password grants the attacker persistent, privileged access to the entire Windows Active Directory domain.

#### _Impacket Extraction Syntax_

To extract Kerberoasting hashes using the Impacket toolset, attackers and security practitioners utilize the Impacket toolset's `GetUserSPNs.py`. This tool authenticates to domain controllers as a standard domain user and queries the Global Catalog (GC) for accounts that have a registered Service Principal Name (SPN). It also automatically identifies targeted user accounts and requests a Kerberos Ticket Granting Service (TGS) ticket for each discovered service.

To execute the extraction, you run the script from a terminal by fist specifying the target domain, valid user credentials, and the IP address of the domain controller to be leveraged for this attack. The command requires a format to be executed such as the one below:

```
GetUserSPNs.py -dc-ip <Domain_Controller_IP> <Domain_Name>/<Username>:<Password> -request
```

Adding the `-request` flag is the critical step that basically tells the script to request the TGS tickets from the Key Distribution Center (KDC) rather than just listing the available SPN accounts enumeration finds.

To save the extracted ticket hashes for offline cracking using tools like John the Ripper, you append an output flag to your command. By adding `outputfile roast_hashes.txt` to the end of your script execution, Impacket will format the cryptographic data into the standard `krb5tgs` structure and will write it directly to the designated file. Once this file is generated, you can transfer it to your cracking rig to perform the brute-force or dictionary-based password guessing attack described previously.

#### Cracking Economics: RC4 Versus AES

The practical gap between Table 13-1's two rows is the entire justification for the defensive posture developed next. An RC4-protected ticket, on commodity GPU hardware available to any well-resourced adversary, can be tested against a large dictionary and rule set in minutes to hours. An AES-protected ticket, subjected to the same wordlist and rule set, can take substantially longer per candidate, to the point that a sufficiently strong underlying password becomes cracking-resistant in any practically useful timeframe. Encryption-type enforcement does not make the account un-Kerberoastable - the TGS-REQ still succeeds, and a ticket is still returned - but it can make the returned material effectively worthless against a reasonably chosen password.

It is worth noting and to be precise about what "reasonably chosen" is carrying in that sentence, because encryption type and password entropy are independent variables that only work together. An eight-character password built from a dictionary word and a short numeric suffix remains crackable under AES given enough time and the right mask or rule set - AES raises the cost per candidate, it does not make every password immune. A long, high-entropy passphrase gains the most benefit from AES enforcement, because the two protections compound: a large candidate space combined with an expensive per-candidate cost pushes the total attack time well past what is operationally useful even for a well-resourced adversary. Encryption-type enforcement is a genuine and most valuable control, but it is also a multiplying force on password strength, not a substitute for it.

### Defensive Architecture: Eliminating the Attack Surface

The defensive answer to Kerberoasting is architectural, not procedural. No amount of password-policy enforcement alone closes this gap while human-managed service account passwords remain the norm; the durable fix replaces the human-chosen secret entirely wherever possible, and raises the cracking cost everywhere it cannot.

#### Group Managed Service Accounts (gMSA)

A Group Managed Service Account (gMSA) carries a 240-bit random password, generated and automatically rotated by Active Directory on an interval you control - 30 days by default - with the credential never known to, or chosen by, a human administrator. Because the password is neither human-generated nor stable long enough to make offline cracking worthwhile, gMSA migration removes the economic basis for Kerberoasting against that account entirely, rather than simply raising the cost. Provisioning a gMSA is a small operation:

```
New-ADServiceAccount -Name svc_sqlreporting -DNSHostName sql01.contoso.mil`
-PrincipalsAllowedToRetrieveManagedPassword "SQL01$"
Install-ADServiceAccount -Identity svc_sqlreporting
```

The migration effort is rarely the PowerShell command itself; it is identifying which application or service can actually consume a gMSA identity without any code changes, which is why a phased inventory - starting with the highest-privileged Kerberoastable accounts identified during target prioritization - is the practical starting point rather than attempting a wholesale cutover.

Two prerequisites trip up first-time gMSA deployments often enough to flag explicitly. A forest must have a KDS root key created before any gMSA can be provisioned, via `Add-KdsRootKey`, and that key takes up to ten hours to propagate across all domain controllers by default when created with its standard effective-time setting - a delay that surprises administrators who create the key and then attempt to provision a gMSA in the same maintenance window and watch it fail.

Application compatibility is the second and larger hurdle: not every legacy application or third-party installed supports gMSA-style managed credentials at the logon layer, and confirming that very support, or planning around its absence, is real security detection engineering work that the migration command itself does not always capture.

#### Enforcing AES-Only Kerberos Encryption

Where migration to a gMSA is not immediately achievable - a legacy application that hard-codes an account and cannot be re-pointed without a vendor engagement is the recurring federal example - restricting the account to AES encryption removes RC4 as an option and forces any Kerberoasting attempt into the far more expensive cracking path. This is actually set per-account through the `msDS-SupportedEncryptionTypes` attribute:

```
Set-ADAccountControl 0Identity svc_legacyapp -Msds-SupportedEncryptionTypes 24
```

A value of `24` restricts the account to AES128 and AES256 only. The number is a bitmask rather than an arbitrary code, and understanding it prevents a common copy-paste mistake: `8` represents AES128 support, `16` represents AES256 support, and the two summed together produce `24` - explicitly excluding the bits that would otherwise enable DES or RC4.

Administrators who only add AES support without also confirming the RC4 bits are absent frequently end up with an account that will support both, which reintroduces exactly the negotiation exposure described earlier in this chapter. This account-level setting works together with, but is distinct from, the domain-wide Group Policy setting, "_Network security: Configure encryption types allowed for Kerberos,"_ found under `Computer Configuration > Windows Settings > Security Settings > Local Policies > Security Options`. The GPO setting governs what the domain controller is willing to negotiate; the account attribute governs what that specific account's key material supports. Both should be configured, and neither substitutes for the other. AES support of this kind has been available since Windows Server 2008 domain functional level, which is a non-issue in virtually every current environment but occasionally worth confirming explicitly in a federal enclave still running a legacy functional level for reasons unrelated to identity security.

#### Service Account Password Policy for Legacy Accounts

For the small remaining population of accounts that can migrate to neither gMSA nor AES enforcement - genuinely rare, but not zero, in mature federal environments running decades-old mission applications - the fallback is long, high-entropy, randomly generated password, twenty-five characters or more, stored in a credential vault rather than committed to memory or documentation, and rotated in a defined schedule rather than left unchanged for years at a stretch.

Where even that fallback cannot be fully implemented on the assessment's timeline - a mission application undergoing its own separate modernization effort is a common federal scenario - the finding shouldn ot simply be left open silently. Documenting the exposure as a compensating control decision in the relevant Risk Management Framework Plan of Action and Milestones (POA\&M) package gives an Authorizing Official (AO) visibility into the accepted risk and a tracked remediation date, which is a materially different posture than an exposure nobody above the assessment team knows exists.

#### Privilege Containment for Security Accounts

Encryption and password strength address whether a Kerberoastable account can be cracked. Privilege containment addresses what happens if it is. A service account that runs a mid-tier application has no legitimate need for Domain Admin membership, local administrative rights across the environment, or unconstrained delegation, and every one of those excess grants converts a single cracked password into a domain-wide compromise rather than a contained one. Tiered administration principles apply directly here: a service account belongs in the tier that matches the systems it operates, not the tier that convenience once assigned it.

In concrete terms, containment means removing the unconstrained delegation flag wherever it is not explicitly required, replacing it with constrained delegation scoped to the specific services the account legitimately needs to reach, and denying interactive and remote desktop logon rights on any service account that has no real legitimate reason to be used for interactive administration in the first place. The built-in Protected Users security group is worth understanding for this purpose but deploying carefully rather than by default: membership disables NTLM and DES/RC4 authentication for the account and blocks credential caching, which closes off several attack pathways at once, but it also caps Kerberos ticket lifetimes at four hours with no renewal period, which can severely disrupt a service account backing a long-running process that was never designed to re-authenticate, mid-session. ### Detection Security Engineering

Because the request itself is legitimate protocol behavior, detection cannot rely on blocking the TGS-REQ. It relies instead on recognizing the pattern that separates a legitimate service ticket request from an enumeration sweep.

#### Event ID 4769 and Ticket Encryption Type

Domain controllers log Event ID 4769, _"A Kerberos service ticket was requested,"_ for every TGS-REQ they process, provided _"Audit Kerberos Service Ticket Operations"_ is enabled for Success events under the _Advanced Audit Policy Configuration, Account Logon_ category. The event carries the requesting account, the target service name, the client address, and the Ticket Encryption field - `0x17` for RC4, `0x12` for AES256, `0x11` for AES128. A `0x17` value against a non-machine account is the first filter worth applying, since it immediately excludes the overwhelming majority of legitimate machine-to-machine Kerberos traffic that this event also captures.

Two operational realities are worth planning around before this event ever reaches a correlation rule.

First, success auditing for Kerberos Service Ticket Operations is not universally enabled out of the box in every environment's baseline - it is a common finding, on its own, to discover that a domain controller is not generating 4769 events at all because nobody explicitly turned the subcategory on.

Second, this event is extraordinarily high volume in any production directory of meaningful size - a busy domain controller can generate many thousands of 4769 events per hour purely from legitimate machine-to-machine service authentication, and a SIEM ingestion pipeline sized for a lower-volume event category will either drop events under load or drive licensing costs upward unexpectedly. Both realities argue for filtering as close to the source as practical rather than shipping every 4769 event downstream unfiltered.

#### Detecting Enumeration Sweeps

The strongest-single behavioral signal is cardinality, not any individual event. A user account requesting service tickets for one or two SPNs across a working day is unremarkable. The same account requesting tickets for dozens for SPNs within a span of seconds to minutes is not something a human performs manually, and it is exactly the signature Rubeus, Impacket, and PowerView all produce when run against a broad target list rather than a single narrowed one. A correlation rule counting distinct Service Name values per requesting account within a rolling short window, filtered to RC4-encrypted requests against non-machine accounts, catches this patterns with a low false-positive rate in most enterprise environments.

That low false-positive rate depends on a baseline that has to be built deliberately rather than assumed. A monitoring platform, a backup solution, or a federation service account can legitimately touch several distinct SPNs as part of normal operations, and a correlation rule deployed straight to production without a preceding baseline period will generate exactly the noisy, ignored alert stream that erodes analyst trust in the detection long-term. A one-to-two-week tuning window, during which the rule fires to a log rather than a paged alert, is enough in most environments to identify and explicitly exclude the small population of legitimate high-cardinality accounts before the rule graduates to production status.

#### Honeypot and Canary Service Principal Name (SPN) Accounts

A deliberately planted decoy account - indistinguishable from a real target in any enumeration output, but never used by any legitimate service - offers close to the highest-fidelity detection available for this technique. Any Event ID 4769 referencing the canary's Service Principal Name is, by construction, illegitimate, since no real workload ever calls it. The account should carry a plausible name and SPN pattern consistent with the environment's naming convention, and the alert pathway should route directly to the security operations center rather than into a general log stream where it competes with the high background volume of ordinary 4769 events.

The naming convention detail matters more than it first appears. A canary account named '`honeypot-do-not-use` or similarly self-describing text defeats its own purpose the moment an adversary reads the enumeration output, since any reasonably careful operator excludes obviously suspicious entries before requesting tickets. An SPN following the exact naming pattern the environment already uses for real accounts - `MSSQLSvc/rpt02.contoso.mil:1433` sitting alongside a genuine `MSSALSvc/rpt01.contoso.mil:1433`, for instance - is what makes the decoy indistinguishable in practice. A canary account is not a deploy-once control, either; it needs periodic review to confirm it has not accidentally been discovered and excluded by name in some internal documentation, and that its alert pipeline still fires correctly, since a canary nobody is actively watching provides absolutely no protection at all despite appearing on every architecture diagram as though it apparently does.

#### SIEM Correlation Logic

Expressed as pseudocode rather than a specific product's query language, since detection platforms vary across federal environments, the enumeration-sweep rule from above reduces to:

```
SELECT RequestingAccount, COUNT(DISTINCT ServiceName) AS distinct_spns
FROM WindowsSecurityEvents
WHERE EventID = 4769
    AND TicketEncryptionType = '0x17'
    AND SErviceName NOT LIKE '%$'
GROUP BY RequestingAccount, TumblingWindow(5 MINUTES)
HAVING dinstinct_spns > = 5
```

The threshold of five distinct SPNs in five minutes is a starting point, not a fixed rule: it should be tuned against each environment's own baseline of legitimate multi-service accounts, such as monitoring or backup solutions that routinely touch several SPNs as part of normal operation, before being promoted from a tuning exercise to a production alert. The canary account rule from the preceding section is consistently simpler, because it needs no threshold or baseline at all - any match is, be definition, an incident.

```
SELECT RequestingAccount, ClienAddress, EventTime
FROM WindowsSecurityEvents
WHERE EventID = 4769
    AND ServiceName = 'MSSQLSvc/rpt02.contoso.mil:1433
```

Both rules should be reviewed on a recurring cycle rather than deployed once and forgotten about, since directory changes over time - new legitimate multi-service accounts provisioned, application migrations, command reshuffling - will eventually push the cardinality rule's false-positive rate back up if its exclusion list is never revisited.

### Federal ICAM-Specific Considerations

Kerberoasting interacts with federal identity architecture in one way that deserves direct correction, because the misunderstanding is common enough to leave real environments exposed.

#### PIV/CAC Authentication Does Not Neutralize Kerberoasting

A Personal Identity Verification (PIV) or Common Access Card (CAC) mandate secures the Authentication Service exchange, and the human user's initial logon, via PKINIT, no longer depends on a password the user knows. It has no bearing whatsoever on the Ticket Granting Service (TGS) exchange that Kerberoasting targets, because that exchange is protected by the target service account's own key, not by anything related to how the requesting human authenticated. An environment can enforce smart-card-only logon for every human user and remain fully exposed to Kerberoasting, because the vulnerable material belongs to the service account, not the person making the actual request. Any assessment or audit that treats a PIV mandate as evidence this exposure is closed is assessing the wrong control.

This is not a criticism of PIV or CAC enforcement, which does exactly what is is designed to do and does it well: it removes phishable and easily guessable human entity passwords from the initial authentication step, which us a substantial and worthwhile improvement to an organization's overall identity posture. The point is narrower and more precise than "smart cards don't work" - it is that PIV and CAC enforcement protects one link in the authentication chain, and Kerberoasting targets a different link entirely, one that has nothing to do with how any humans in the environment logs on. A mature assessment credits PIV enforcement for exactly what it secures and evaluates Kerberoasting exposure as a completely separate question, rather than treating the two as a single combined control.

#### Rick Management Framework (RMF) and Security Technical Implementation Guide (STIG)

Kerberoasting exposure maps most directly to the Risk Management Framework's Authenticator Management (IA-5) and Least Privilege (AC-6) control families - IA-5 through the strength and management of the service account credential itself, AC-6 through the privilege containment discussed earlier in this chapter that limits the consequence of a successful crack. Continuous monitoring activities under System Monitoring (SI-4) and Audit Review, Analysis, and Reporting (AU-6) should include the detection logic developed earlier in this chapter as a named control, not an incidental byproduct of general log collection. Operation System STIG baselines address kerberos encryption-type configuration directly; the specific control identifier and current revision should be verified against the live STIG at assessment time rather than cited from memory, since STIG content is revised on its own schedule independent of this book's publication date.

Where a finding cannot be closed within the assessment window - the AES-migration timeline for a legacy application awaiting vendor support is the recurring example - RMF documentation practice matters as much as the technical fix itself. A finding tracked as an open Plan of Action and Milestones (POA\&M) item, with a named owner, a targeted remediation date for closure, and the specific control family it maps to, gives an Authroizing Official (AO) something concrete to weigh during the risk acceptance decision. A finding mentioned only in an assessment report's prose and never carried into the POA\&M tends to be the one that is still open, unaddressed, and forgotten when the next continuous monitoring cycle is established.

#### Assessment Checklist for Federal Environments

* Enumerate every account carrying a Service Principal Name (SPN) and cross-reference against `adminCount` and current privileged group membership.
* Confirm `msDS-SupportedEncryptionTypes` for every privileged Kerberoastable account excludes RC4.
* Confirm Audit Kerberos Service ticket Operations success auditing is enabled on all domain controllers.
* Verify a distinct-SPN-count corrleation rule exists and has been fine-tuned and tailored against the environment's legitiamte multi-service accounts.
* Confirm no assessment or authorization package cites smart card enforcement as a mitigating control for this exposure.

### Case Study: Kerberoasting to Domain Admin

_Case Study 13-1. Kerberoasting to Domain Admin Through an Unmonitored Legacy SQL Service Account_

During an authorized assessment of a federal mission network, enumeration surfaced eleven accounts carrying Service Principal Names (SPNs). Cross-referencing `adminCount` identified one, `svc_sql_reporting`, as having once held Domain Admin membership; current membership showed only a locally scoped database role, but the historical flag was enough to warrant prioritization ahead of the other ten. A Rubeus request further narrowed to that single account, using the `/rc4opsec` flag to confirm it still supported RC4, returned a ticket within seconds. Offline cracking against a standard wordlist and rule set recovered the plaintext password in under four minutes - a seasonal reference paired with a two-digit year, a pattern consistent with a password chosen once at account creation and never revisited.

The account's current permissions, though reduced from its Domain Admin history, still carried local administrative rights on three database servers and an unconstrained delegation flag left over from an earlier migration project - precisely the kind of residual grant this chatper's privilege-containment guidance addresses. From those three servers, cached credentials belonging to a currently privileged administrator were recoverable, closing the pathway back to full domain compromise. The finding was reported with two separate remediation tracks: migrate the account to a Group Managed Service Account (gMSA) outright, since the reporting application it supported had no hard dependency preventing the change, and remove the unconstrained delegation flag immediately regardless of the migration timeline, since that flag represented independent risk unrelated to the password strength that had made the initial compromise possible.

The finding was rated Critical rather than High, a distinction worth explaining because it recurs across this kind of chained exposure: neither the weak password alone nor the unconstrained delegation flag alone would have justified a critical rating on its own; however, the combination - a crackable credential feeding directly into a pathway back to full domain compromise - is exactly the kind of chained risk that a purely checklist-driven assessment tends to underrate, since each individual finding looks moderate in isolation. The unconstrained delegation removal shipped within the week, requiring no application downtime. The gMSA migration took closer to six weeks, most of it spent confirming that the reporting application's connection-string configuration could actually authenticate using a managed identity without a code change, which is the ordinary shape migration work takes in a live mission environment rather than in an out-of-band lab environment.

### Key Takeaways

In this chapter, you learned:

* Kerberoasting exploits Kerberos as designed, not a misconfiguration - any authenticated account can request a ticket for any Service Principal Name (SPN).
* The service ticket is encrypted with a key derived from the target account's own password, making the request valuable only when that password is weak or human-chosen, or provided.
* Computer accounts are technically Kerberoastable but rarely worth pursuing; human-managed service accounts are the practical target.
* RC4-encrypted tickets crack orders of magnitude faster than AES-encrypted tickets, making encryption-type enforcement a meaningful interim control.
* Group Managed Service Accounts (gMSAs) remove the exploitable password entirely and are the durable fix wherever migration is feasible.
* Detection cannot block the request itself; it must recognize the cardinality pattern of an enumeration sweep and can be sharpened with canary SPN accounts.
* Smart card and PIV/CAC authentication secures human logon and has no effect on Kerberoasting, which targets the service account's key independent of how the requester authenticated.
* Privilege containment on service accounts limits the consequence of a successful crack even when the password itself cannot yet be hardened.<br>
